# DEMON FIGHTER — Chronicle: The Breach of Antarkeia
### REVISED 2026-09-11 — Antem-Extinction Overhaul pass

The founding event of the 0 YOC calendar — the day a coalition of Faerl houses broke into a vault nobody living had ever seen opened, and the ten lives caught inside it.

As with everything in this project: breadcrumbs, not canon set in stone. This document supersedes the earlier "Fall of Antarkeia" battle version in full — there was no battle, and no living defenders. What follows replaces that account, not supplements it.

## THE BREACH OF ANTARKEIA
*A generation after the Great Rivening's aftermath had settled into fragments and legend — Year Zero of the YOC calendar*

By the time the four banners reached Antarkeia, there was no one left to defend it. The Antem had been gone for thousands of years — Hollowed, Rivened, and scattered long before any Faerl house existed with the reach to march this far. What the coalition found was not a fortress under siege but a vault nobody had opened in longer than anyone could reckon: sealed gates, dust-choked halls, and whatever had been left behind to keep them that way.

### What Was Left Behind

Nothing here was alive, and nothing here was harmless. Cragwings nested in the high broken stone above the old gathering-grounds, undisturbed for longer than any living memory could account for. Deeper in, where the old stonework gave way to something no one on the expedition could fully explain, a scattering of wraiths were said to drift the passages — not summoned, not commanded, simply there, the way a haunted place accumulates its own weather over enough centuries. Deeper still, sealed doors answered to mechanisms that still worked, in whatever cold and patient way a machine can be said to work after five thousand years with no one to maintain it. None of it recognized the coalition as anything but an intrusion. None of it needed to.

Older Antem pictographs — the handful the coalition would later recover — show this place had always had its watchers, long before any living memory of who or what had set them there.

### The Refusal at Emberfall

The last recorded attempt at a negotiated approach before the four houses marched: representatives of Thorne, Dray, and Colehurst met at Emberfall to discuss dividing whatever the expedition found without turning on each other over it, and were joined, unexpectedly, by a Marlow envoy proposing something none of the other three wanted to hear — that whatever remained of the Antem, in relic, ruin, or grave-good, be formally preserved and catalogued rather than simply divided and carried off. The other three refused outright, not out of any particular malice the record bothers preserving, but out of simple arithmetic: a formal preservation claim meant less for everyone else to take. The envoy was sent home. Marlow's own river-lords, delayed at a silted crossing during the march itself, would arrive at Antarkeia last of all four banners — a coincidence no one in the moment connected to the table they'd been turned away from, though at least one later Marlow chronicler couldn't resist noting that arriving late to matter, for this house, was becoming something close to tradition.

### The Gathering of the Four

The coalition itself was arithmetic, not trust — Maren Thorne's deep-forest holds, Torvin Dray's desert clans, Harroc Colehurst's fire-forges and his bound wizards, whose fire-lance volleys are said to have turned the sky the color of the forge before anyone had even reached the outer gate. Denna Marlow's river-lords were delayed at a silted crossing, and Thorne, Dray, and Colehurst reached Antarkeia without her.

They had expected a dead legend, not immediate danger. What they found, past the outer gate, was a place that fought back without anyone inside it choosing to — collapsing passages, harpy assaults on anyone who lingered near the heights, ward-mechanisms that woke as deeper seals were disturbed. The effort to breach the inner vault very nearly failed on the ninth day, not to any defender, but to the ground itself. Colehurst's own dispatches record it plainly: *we did not ask whether the place would let us through. We asked how much it would cost us to make it.*

### The River-Lords Arrive, and What It Cost

Marlow's banners reached the site on the morning of the eleventh day, her engineers finally cracking the mechanism that had held the innermost seal against nine days of failed attempts by the other three houses. What followed was not one dramatic breakthrough but the better part of a day of controlled collapse — floors giving way, sealed chambers venting air that had not moved in millennia, an entire wing of the old gathering-hall folding in on itself as whatever ancient logic had held it upright finally lost the fight. It is this collapse — sudden, total, and utterly indifferent to who was standing where — that swallowed the deepest search parties and the relics they were carrying alike.

The Antarkeia relics lost in that collapse have never been recovered.

One more sighting belongs here: on the last morning, a lone Non was seen on the high ground above the gathering-place, watching, gone before anyone thought to approach. No two tellings agree on whether it was an omen for the coalition or something closer to a witness paying respects.

A named piece of that same last morning deserves its place here too. Cael Thorncrest's tally from the ninth day survives in three mismatched copies, and every history that mentions it treats the gaps as an artifact of a boy overwhelmed by more dead than any child should count. A minority tradition disagrees. It holds that Cael's count didn't fail — it stopped, deliberately, on the last morning, and that whatever he saw in the final collapse was not a number he had any interest in adding to the others.

He never said what it was. What survives is secondhand, gathered years later by the same chronicler who admits he arrived four days too late to have witnessed any of it himself — likely the only reason any version of this survived at all. In it, something moved through the wreckage where the old stonework gave way to the part of the grounds no one had ever fully explained. It was seen and gone before anyone standing could agree on what they'd seen — low light, red-shot, the wrong shape for anything that should have been moving in a place already this full of dead and dust. It did not fight. Nothing that saw it reported fighting it. It was simply, briefly, undeniably there, and then it wasn't, and the handful who saw it spent the rest of their lives being believed by no one and doubted by most, including, some nights, themselves.

It is this — not the coalition's dead, not the collapse itself — that the lone Non on the high ground is now more often read as having come to witness. Whatever he understood, watching from above while the last of what the vault had been keeping quiet finally came loose, no account credits him with surprise.

### What Antarkeia Became

There was no army to disperse and no surrender to record, because there was no one left to surrender. What the coalition took from Antarkeia was proof — the first anyone had ever verified firsthand — that the legendary Antem had been real, and were now, beyond any remaining doubt, entirely gone. The scattered relics that survived the final collapse left the site the moment the coalition dispersed, carried outward by whoever had reached them first, and never regrouped; it is why fragments of clearly pre-Faerl make keep surfacing across Faerlan to this day, in hands that have no idea what they're holding or where it came from.

The coalition that breached Antarkeia never became one kingdom. Four powers who could not fully trust each other through eleven days of shared danger were never going to govern anything together in peace — and beyond that, none of the four ever fully claimed the ground itself. It had been Antem land, and legend, for too long and too visibly for any single banner to plant itself there without answering to the other three. What Antarkeia became, in the years after, was neither conquest nor kingdom — just ground everyone had reason to leave alone, and a vault nobody has ever felt the need to reseal.

It is this day the Faerl count as the beginning of everything after — not because a war was won, but because it was the day legend became verified fact, and the day whatever the Antem had spent millennia containing here stopped being anyone's problem to manage. Before Antarkeia, there is only the long, unmeasured chaos of the Scattering Wars and a half-believed story about a vanished people. After it, for the first time, there is a year worth naming Year Zero — and the world's oldest ghost story turned out to be true.

## Ten Who Were There

*Wiki-stub length, on purpose — each one implies a fuller story rather than tells it.*

**Cael Thorncrest — the boy who counted the dead wrong.** A twelve-year-old drummer-boy attached to Thorne's forest levies, tasked with tallying the losses from the ninth day's collapse and harpy assaults. His count survives in three mismatched copies. A minority tradition holds he stopped counting on purpose — see "The River-Lords Arrive, and What It Cost" above for the fuller account of what that might mean.

**The Silent Company.** A party of some forty coalition sappers who vanished into the deep passages three days before the river-lords arrived, and reappeared two nights later inside a rival house's supply column, wearing colors that weren't theirs. The wagons they moved never reached where they were meant to.

**Roswen Ashcairn — the smith who never swung a blade.** A camp armorer with Colehurst's fire-forges, credited with reworking a cracked fire-lance mount that held light and heat against the deep-passage wraiths through the ninth day. Died of exhaustion three days after the breach, never mentioned in Colehurst's own dispatches.

**Beric Hallowmere — first to fall.** A Dray-sworn desert rider, already a father of three with two unremarkable decades behind him, killed by the very first trap sprung at the outer gate. His sons founded a minor holding that still bears his name.

**Ythrin, called the Unspent.** A Colehurst vanguard scout said to have walked the deepest galleries for all eleven days without retreat or serious injury, then simply walked away once the innermost seal finally broke. Faerl accounts call it cowardice; others call it the harder duty of having seen enough to want none of the spoils.

**The Marlow twins.** Denna Marlow's own siblings, said to have led her relief column into the breach together on the eleventh day — one credited with the largest confirmed haul of recovered relics in any surviving account, the other never recorded again after that morning's collapse.

**Pell Dunmarrow — the deserter's name that became a curse.** A river-lord conscript who fled on the eve of the breach. Three different fates are recorded for him. "Pulling a Dunmarrow" is still the insult you'd guess it is.

**Sarel of the Long Watch.** A Faerl conscript, sworn to no house in particular, who held a narrow chokepoint alone for an afternoon after the rest of her company was lost to a passage collapse — armed with a spear that reportedly wasn't hers, and had no business being anywhere near the surface world before that day. Likely namesake of the modern idiom "holding the long watch."

**Orune Featherfall — the harpy that didn't attack.** Cragwings screamed at every approaching party except, several accounts claim, one scouting group on the sixth day. Its leader called it luck. Antem pictographs, where they survive, call it something closer to a verdict.

**The Chronicler Who Wasn't There.** Author of the single most-cited account of Antarkeia's final days, who admits in his own closing lines that he arrived four days after the breach ended and reconstructed everything from survivors. His name is lost; only the account remains.
