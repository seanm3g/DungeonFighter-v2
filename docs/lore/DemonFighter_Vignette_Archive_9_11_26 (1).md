**DEMON FIGHTER**

*The Vignette Archive*

*Joey's four originals, followed by four companion vignettes drafted to match, spanning multiple distance-bands of Faerlan history.*

*As with everything in this project: these are breadcrumbs, not canon set in stone. Placement, names, and details are all*

*open to revision as the wider history develops around them.*

**PART ONE — THE ORIGINALS**

**The Seventh Keeping**

*An observed ritual, once a generation, ongoing into the present era*

The Seventh Keeping is a ritualistic trial observed by mountain-dwelling Faerl. It happens once a generation. During the trial, as a rite of passage, the seven mountain clans each offer a champion whose duty it is to take and keep the Vista of the Shimmering Mound, a relatively small mountain whose top appears to be completely shorn off. It provides a sprawling view of the countryside for leagues. While it is nowhere near the tallest known mountain, it is noteworthy because it no longer has a peak, just a flat prairie, roughly a mile in diameter at its top — as if the mountain's peak was leveled by some tremendous impact millennia ago. In the center of the prairie is a lake said to shimmer like diamonds during certain moon cycles. Men who have held the Vista are said to grow beyond the size of normal men. It is said they never need to eat — that drinking from the lake is all they need to survive.

The journey to the top of the Vista is arduous, with many champions thought to perish to exposure, injury, or encounters with wild beasts before cresting the summit. If none survive, the Seventh Keeping is still considered complete for that generation. If several champions crest the summit, it is said that the rapture they feel upon arrival causes them to grow envious, insatiable, which leads to a survival-of-the-fittest competition from which only one emerges. That single individual must then do battle with the Seventh Keeper, the current overseer of the Vista. If they best the overseer, they become the new Seventh Keeper; if they lose, the next generation tries again. It is said that the Seventh Keeper never abandons their watch — scholars note that it is impossible to know who the current Seventh Keeper is, as none of the champions ever return home.

Antem, who once piloted large silver winged hawks — a species extinct for thousands of years — have legends of being drawn to the prairie while flying over it, almost magnetically, but that when their hawks attempted to land, they were repelled and thrown to the craggy rocks below. Before their hawks went extinct during the Great Rivening, Antem legends claim to have witnessed mostly lumbering male giants, but there are also legends of female Keepers.

**The Widow's Accords**

*The Widow's War — ratified in the 1st century of the War, held primacy roughly 50 years*

The Widow's Accords were deliberated on and ratified in the 1st century of the Widow's War. After decades of brutal warfare, strife, and calamity that sapped the continent of nearly eighty percent of its war-ready men, the countryside was in a shambles. Since there were fewer men to hunt, forage, build, and most importantly reproduce, entire regions' populations began to wither and die. In an attempt to bring a peaceful close to the conflict, a great council was convened that drew together a group of one hundred women from all corners of the continent, known only as “The Widows.” They ranged in age from ten years old, who had not even begun to bleed, to old crones who had ceased bleeding long ago.

For a year, these women discussed and deliberated a path forward. As peace was elusive, they discussed establishing a progressive matriarchy — the few men left alive would be refused breeding rights if they participated in the Widow's War. Those who settled down and began to contribute more to society than simple warfaring were allowed entry into the matriarchal structure, although due to their brute sensibilities they were considered inferior to the women. Since there were so few men left on the continent, the matriarchy was able to make some real progress in the following fifteen years of relative calm, although warring continued throughout the continent in varying degrees. They built impressive structures and established strong trade routes — the Widow's Blush, a massive forest that remains largely unsullied to this day, was preserved specifically because the Widows had the foresight to establish vital trading routes through it, preventing its destruction or compromise.

Ultimately the Widow's Accords held primacy for some fifty years, enough time to bring relative peace to the continent and repopulate the male of the species. Once the male population was almost back to parity with the women, however, a great revolt was launched by Beghar Flintbrow, a brutal and murderous despot said to be the descendant of a demon himself — although surviving histories from the matriarchy identify him as a petty prince and son of a high-ranking matriarchal lady. It is said that while ranging in a distant desert zone, Beghar changed — returning full of swagger and bravado, he was initially charming to the leaders of the matriarchy. Some even said they could see a warm amber glow in the back of his pupils that seemed new and alien. Over the span of a decade, though, his charm deteriorated into some sort of hatred and vitriol. He dug up old stories about the matriarchy and spun them into lessons for men who he claimed were cuckolded, weakened, entitled to their birthright of sex. As his power grew, it is said the amber color of his eyes deepened. Within a year, the matriarchy was crushed, with women returning to their marginalized positions in the hierarchy. To this day there are small sects, akin to nunneries, that still observe the tenets of the Widow's Accords — Faerl society views them with some reverence but also suspicion. The control they assert over politics and trade gives them a privileged position, but they are a mere shade of their former glory.

**The Boy Who Counted Stars**

*Charter Centuries (exact date not yet pinned)*

The Boy Who Counted Stars, true name Flynn Ofshalleiu, was a highborn noble and the only son of warlord Ogar Ofshalleiu. He had thirteen sisters. A feeble boy, he was said to have been born without the use of his body from the neck down — some suggest a congenital defect, others that Ogar, in his excitement and haste at having finally sired a male heir, hastily pulled the child from his mother, thus creating a fracture in his spine that led to his affliction. As Flynn would be of no use on the battlefield, Ogar found little use for him outside of using astrology to improve the petty kingdom's knowledge of seasons and shipfaring — so he would sit the boy out nightly, cocking his neck backward, and force him to observe the stars' movements. The Boy Who Counted Stars is now widely seen as the most influential scientific mind to have existed in modernity — some noble houses in the modern era consider him one of the most formidable scientists and observers in all of history, raising him to legendary status amongst other figures from Faerlan's sagas and myths. It was his sworn servant, a quiet man of no particular house, who wrote down his observations and committed them to text.

Amongst his observations, Flynn was the first to perceive and describe the Black Mun — old Antem legend told of a monolith that seemed to monitor the planet from the sky, though no Faerl before him had ever had cause or means to look for it. Flynn, with his savant-like understanding of the night sky, was able to triangulate its positioning and use it to predict such things as ecological disasters, seasonal ebbs and flows, and some even say the future. He claimed that on occasion he would see the Black Mun flicker, a claim even his own household found hard to credit.

Flynn's own star burned bright and fast — by the age of fourteen he was afflicted with incurable sores that covered his entire body. By the time of his passing, it is said that Flynn's body was so rotted that, were you to remove his fur cloak, all that would remain would be a frail, skin-covered head resting atop a skeleton. In spite of his early death, all modern seafaring and astrological science is built around his observations. The line of Ofshalleiu — thought to be cursed due to Ogar's impatience and Flynn's almost supernatural ability to see into the deepest mysteries of the known cosmos — disappeared from Faerlan.

**Kolek's Manuscript — The Unmaking of Grael's Promise**

*Frame: early Scattering Wars, outside Antarkeia. The parable within: origin unknown, deep antiquity*

During the early years of the Scattering Wars, while ranging deep within the forests outside of Antarkeia, an ancient manuscript was discovered by regional chieftain Kolek Tymtrove. Kolek and his men, desperate for food after ignorantly overhunting the forest grounds, resorted to eating trees, moss, and even rocks to survive. While digging through a rotten tree seeking grubs, Kolek claimed that his axe blade began to burn white hot, and that the heat of the blade, which he promptly threw to the ground, bore a hole twenty feet through the forest floor, exposing an entrance to an “iron-encrusted” cavern. The exact location of this iron cavern is lost to history, as when Kolek tried to bring witnesses back to the site it was nowhere to be found — but in his accounts, Kolek reported seeing shapes and rare minerals he had never seen before in his life. He recounted trying to touch them, but his wavering hand did nothing more than cause them to burst into dust. After hours of wandering and reckless, ignorant destruction of what may have been prehistoric artifacts, Kolek claimed to enter a room full of fresh vegetables, fruit, meats, cheeses, and fresh water. Placed gently in the middle of this great bounty was a manuscript, made of some material Kolek could not identify. He thought he had lost his mind.

The manuscript, etched deep into the odd material and nestled into a pile of dust — as if the weight of this particular piece devastated the other stories beneath it — was written in some strange, ancient derivative of Faerlish, and told the parable: The Unmaking of Grael's Promise.

The parable, in summary, told the story of Grael, an old spinster who lived in a small hamlet where few spoke closely or personally outside of exchanges of goods and resources. Her specialty was shepherding Kauwto herds. One day Grael was greeted by a roaming Vessel carrying a bundle in its arms. The Vessel asked her to keep a secret — within the bundle it revealed a newborn babe, an angelic creature, seemingly not of this world. Its skin looked almost too pure, too white, too clean. It was perfect. The Vessel said that if she would take the babe and raise it as her own, in eighteen years' time the Vessel would return, and as thanks, Grael would be given divine understanding of this realm and the next. The only catch was that the babe could never leave Grael's property, or else certain doom would befall Grael and the child. Grael, intrigued, accepted the offer. She raised the babe as her own, and it showed remarkable strength, intelligence, kindness, and compassion as it aged. This creature was almost like a god in the way it raised its own station and Grael's simultaneously.

Unfortunately, as Grael grew more frail, it became impossible for her to assist with shepherding the herds. Her crops began to fail and her home began to fall apart. Anxious to receive her gift from the Vessel, with only one year left until the child reached eighteen, Grael chose extreme measures. The child did its best to assist, but ultimately Grael needed more help to stay alive that one more year. Breaking her promise, Grael sent the child to town to seek more assistance. The entire town was aghast at his countenance — tall, handsome, otherworldly, almost more god than Faerl. The child brought back three men. The first stole what little food and money Grael had left; the second violated her and left her for dead; the third killed the child and cut him open to see his insides. While he was built just like a Faerl, his blood was a slightly deeper tinge of red. All three men drank of the child's blood and were struck dead as their throats burnt out from the inside. On the day of the child's planned eighteenth birthday, Grael walked out of her house to see the Vessel on the hilltop, about half a mile from her home. He seemed to already know what had happened. He hung his head and left Grael, never to be seen again.

Leaving the manuscript as is, Kolek had his fill of food, then left the cavern to find his troops so he could lead them back to this land of plenty. When he returned, the entrance was gone forever. The existence of the parable and the tale it contained was dictated by Kolek to his learned cleric, who wrote the story down so that future generations could reflect upon it. Kolek died of consumption less than six months later.

**PART TWO — COMPANION VIGNETTES**

**The Keeper Who Would Not Open the Door**

*Deep antiquity, unnumbered*

Beneath the roots of the mountain the Seven Clans would not name the Vista for another age yet, there was a Non known only by the office she held, since no one still living remembers what she was called before it: the Keeper of the Low Door. What she kept behind that door, no one agrees on — a room, a well, a sleeping thing, a single word too dangerous to be spoken twice. What is agreed on is that another Non came to her, after a journey long enough that his robe had worn through at both knees, and asked to be let in.

He did not come as an enemy. He came as a colleague, carrying his own half of something — fragments, pieces, the beginnings of a record he wanted joined to whatever she was guarding, so that between the two halves something whole might finally exist again. She refused him. Not out of malice, the story insists on this part specifically, but out of a conviction that a whole truth, reassembled too soon, in the wrong hands, in the wrong age, would do more harm than the two broken halves ever could apart. He argued that incompleteness was its own kind of harm — that a people who cannot even ask the right questions are not being protected, they are being left blind. She did not disagree with him. She simply did not open the door.

He is said to have sat outside it for six days before he left. He never returned. Some Non tellings hold that he found another door somewhere else, and opened it himself, and that this is the actual reason no two archives among the Non ever fully agree with one another to this day — not decay, not accident, but a choice made once, at the root of everything, by two people who both believed they were protecting the world and could not agree on how.

**The Charter of Salt and Silence**

*Charter Centuries, roughly a generation after the fall of the Widow's Accords*

House Dravencourt held a strip of storm-battered coast that fed no one well and defended poorly, and its lord, Aldous Dravencourt, decided the only way his name would outlast his lifetime was to reach further than any coastal warlord before him had bothered to. He had heard, third- and fourth-hand, of the Vantry sailors — men said to navigate by a knowledge of the water itself rather than the stars, a claim every landbound listener assumed was exaggeration. Aldous funded a single ship, the Second Chance, crewed by forty men who owed him debts they couldn't otherwise repay, and a charter promising each of them a stake in whatever trade route they opened.

The Second Chance sailed out past sight of Faerlan and was not seen again for nineteen years.

What returned was not the ship. It was one man, alone, in a boat he could not name the make of, speaking a dialect close enough to Faerlish to be understood but strange enough that listeners swore he paused before certain words like he was translating them out of something else first. He gave his name as Corwin Ashe, one of the original forty, and he would not say what had happened to the other thirty-nine, only that the Vantry Isles were real, that they were not one land but many scattered across water that never seemed to end, and that whatever he had seen there had convinced him the sea itself was better left alone by men who didn't understand what they were asking it for. He lived another nine years in a fishing hamlet under a different name and never sailed again, not even to fish, and would go pale and silent if anyone asked him directly what the charter's other thirty-nine men had wanted that the sea decided they couldn't have.

House Dravencourt, having spent everything it had on a single ship and gained nothing but one broken sailor and a debt it could never repay, was absorbed by its neighbors within the decade. Its name persists only as a phrase — a Dravencourt charter — used by merchants ever since to mean any deal so lopsided in its promise that only a desperate man would sign it.

**The Boneyard Reckoning**

*Roughly thirty years after Consolidation*

For the first thirty years of settled Faerl kingdoms, demons were still handled the way they always had been — by whoever was unlucky enough to be nearby when one surfaced, usually a local strongman, a hedge-warrior, or a village simply abandoning the ground and hoping the thing stayed put. That changed in the reign of the petty-king Ossian Vale, after a season in which four separate holds along his border were emptied within the same summer and nothing resembling an army could be assembled fast enough to matter.

Ossian's solution was not glory-seeking. It was administrative. He offered land, coin, and — crucially — legal exemption from every debt and crime a man had committed before taking the offer, to anyone willing to be counted, trained under a single banner, and sent wherever the reports came from fastest. History remembers this as cynical, and Ossian himself seems to have known it was cynical; his own court records, the ones that survive, refer to the enlisted men plainly as “the debts and the desperate,” not as heroes. Almost none of the first cohort had fought a demon before. Most of what became doctrine — how to read a room's environmental tells before a fight breaks out, how a status effect that lands on the wrong target can cost more than it saves, the simple discipline of counting your own party before and after — was written in blood by men who did not survive learning it, and passed down by the ones who did.

The order took its name from where it was first quartered: an old boneyard outside Ossian's seat, chosen because no one else wanted the land and the dead already buried there weren't likely to complain about the company. Within two generations, “Boneyard-trained” stopped being an insult and became the closest thing the fractured kingdoms had to a shared professional standard — the first time demon-hunting stopped being something that happened to a place and started being something a person could simply be.

**Dask and Wrenna**

*Age of Myths — contested, roughly contemporary with the Ald and Shek cycle, though no telling agrees on which came first*

Where Ald found a blade and Shek a rod, the story goes that two others found their own gifts on the same night, in a different cave, on the far side of whatever mountain range separated them from the twins' story — though some tellings insist it was the very same cave, visited twice, decades apart, and that this is simply how legends duplicate themselves when no one thought to write anything down the first time.

Dask was said to be a butcher, enormous even before anything happened to him, who struck a buried, humming thing with a cleaver meant for livestock and felt something answer the blow back up through his own arm — not pain, but recognition, like the tool had been waiting its whole existence to finally hit something worth hitting. He never spoke much after. What he did instead was work: clearing land no one else would clear, breaking ground no one else could break, and eventually turning that same enormous, patient strength on anything that came up out of the dark to threaten the people he'd quietly decided were his to look after, without ever being asked to.

Wrenna, in the same telling, was a thief — the story is not shy about this, doesn't dress it up as anything nobler — caught mid-theft in a collapsed shrine by something that should have killed her and instead pressed a narrow blade into her hand before she could even scream. She kept doing exactly what she'd always done: taking what wasn't hers, moving where she wasn't wanted, except now what she stole, more often than not, was other people's dying, taken from them a half-second before it would have landed. She never called it heroism. By every surviving account she found the word faintly insulting.

Neither Dask nor Wrenna is said to have fought for a hundred years the way Ald and Shek did. Their stories end, instead, the way most real lives do — quietly, off the page, with no one quite able to say afterward what became of them, which is either a sign that the tale is younger and less polished than the twins', or proof of the opposite: that it's

old enough to have forgotten its own ending entirely.
