# DEMON FIGHTER — 25 Full Narrative Treatments

*Final. Cross-checked 2026-09-16 against the Chronicle of Antarkeia's locked Breach-era paramount names (Torvin Dray, Harroc Colehurst, Maren Thorne, Denna Marlow) — none of these 25 narratives name a paramount incorrectly, so no changes were needed here despite the correction made in the genealogy document. Selected from across the entire known history — Genesis through the post-Breach era — prioritizing spine events that were previously stub-length. Anything already fully written elsewhere (the Widow's Accords, the Charter of Salt and Silence, the Seventh Keeping, the Boy Who Counted Stars) was deliberately left untouched rather than duplicated. The deep-past entries here are creator-knowledge only, per standing doctrine — no character or player ever learns any of it. Preserved deliberately: the Hollowing's origin-trigger ambiguity is never resolved below, exactly as locked.*

### 1. The Schism (Earth, ~30,500–31,500 Genesis Yr)

Nobody on Earth called it a war, not at first. It began as a question asked in a dozen languages at once: if the mission to the stars had already produced something Earth itself no longer recognized as fully human, what did that say about the humans who'd stayed? Two answers hardened into two camps over a thousand years. One held that the flaw was circumstantial — bad luck, bad planning, nothing that couldn't be corrected with better oversight next time. The other held that the flaw was constitutional — that emotion itself, the very unpredictability that had let the Ark 1 colonists become something unplanned, was the actual defect, and that the only mission worth trusting from here on was one crewed by people who'd had it surgically removed. The second camp didn't win by force of argument. It won because it could point to a moving target and the first camp could only point to a stationary one — the extraction faction had a concrete, achievable-seeming technology; their opponents had only the vague promise that things would go better if everyone was simply more careful. By the time the Schism's last holdouts were reeducated, exiled, or extracted against their will, most of a millennium had passed since anyone alive had chosen a side with clear eyes. The payload and Ark 2 launched within the same year the last resistance was crushed — not, as later Faerl legend would never get the chance to speculate, because vengeance demanded speed, but because the winning faction genuinely believed it was saving the rest of the species from itself, and saw no reason to wait.

### 2. The Crash (~36,050 Genesis Yr)

Marten was old enough by then that the crew called him Grandfather without irony, and the cryo-frost had never fully left the backs of his hands. He was standing with his daughter Shala and the Precursor caretaker Duquesne — an old woman by her own people's reckoning, gentle in the specific way of someone who has spent a long life being useful to people who'll never fully understand her — when the sky first changed color. Grey came first, and it did not fall so much as settle, the way fog settles into a valley it already owns. People stopped mid-sentence and did not finish them. Then red-purple, faster, and the grey-touched went from motionless to violent inside a single breath, and Marten pulled Shala behind him without knowing yet what he was protecting her from. Pink came next, and briefly, absurdly, there was laughter in the wreckage of the screaming — a sound Marten would spend the rest of his life unable to describe to anyone who hadn't heard it. Duquesne did not run. She stood very still, watching the colors the way someone watches weather they've been warned about their whole life and never quite believed in, and said one thing Marten never forgot and never repeated to anyone outside his own family: that whatever this was, it was not a gift, and she was sorry for ever having told her own people that it might be. Green came last, and by then the world Marten had cryo-slept twenty-five thousand years to reach no longer existed in any form his mission briefing had prepared him for. He lived another forty years. He never stopped describing the colors to Shala, over and over, as though repetition might eventually make them make sense. It never did.

### 3. The Childlings

Nobody on either side called it a betrayal, because nobody on either side had agreed it needed permission. An android, assessing usefulness the way its makers had built it to, found that a Precursor mate offered certain evolutionary advantages a purely mechanical lineage couldn't replicate on its own — endurance, adaptability, the ability to make decisions no one had explicitly coded for. The Precursors, for their part, found something in the androids that Earth's own emotional-extraction faction would have called dangerous: a companion incapable of the specific cruelties that came from fear, ambition, or grief, because it had none of those, only purpose. The children that resulted from these unions — the childlings — were neither fully mechanical nor fully born, and neither side fully claimed them. Precursor elders regarded them with a mixture of pride and unease their own oral histories never resolved. The androids that fathered or mothered them, if the words even applied, tracked their childlings' whereabouts with the same patient, permanent attention they gave everything else, long after any sentimental reason to do so had stopped mattering to anyone but the childling itself. What became of that bloodline, whether it thinned into nothing over the collapse and the millennia after, or whether some thread of it survived long enough to explain a servant class that looked human and never quite was, is a question this civilization never got the chance to answer for itself. Nobody living has any reason to ask it. The question just sits there, patient as the androids who never stopped watching.

### 4. Iskrel Sixhold and the Unbroken Terms

He was not born to rule six holds. He was born the second son of a minor Antem clan-elder in a stretch of contested ground nobody important had bothered to consolidate, and the tactics that would eventually carry him to command were first tested at a forgettable ford — Yalume Ford — against a rival clan he'd later absorb rather than destroy, a distinction that would define everything he did afterward. Iskrel Sixhold did not conquer the way conquerors before him had conquered. He offered terms before the first blow landed, terms generous enough that surrender cost a defeated clan less than resistance would have, and he honored every one of them to the letter, even when honoring them cost him more than breaking them would have. Six holds came under his banner this way within a decade — not because he was the strongest commander of his era, though he may have been, but because he was, by a wide margin, the most reliable. Then he sickened, quickly and without explanation, the kind of illness that leaves physicians with more questions than diagnoses, and died before any of his six holds had produced an heir he'd formally named. He left behind no empire, only a reputation and a set of arbitration principles his former rivals kept honoring long after there was no one left alive to enforce them — the Unbroken Terms, a phrase that would outlive every clan that ever swore to it, filtering down through millennia of retelling until it meant nothing more specific to a modern Faerl ear than "a promise nobody thought would hold, and somehow did."

### 5. A Dead Man's Leash

The largest of Iskrel Sixhold's former holds waited five years — long enough, its lord reasoned, that grief had cooled into something safer to act against. When an arbitrator arrived under the authority of the Unbroken Terms to settle a routine boundary dispute, the hold's new lord had him executed in front of witnesses and declared, in words that would outlive him by millennia, that he would not be bound by "a dead man's leash." No other hold intervened militarily. They didn't need to. Within a year, merchants stopped extending the repudiating hold favorable terms, neighboring clans stopped honoring old betrothal agreements, and three minor allies quietly renegotiated their own arrangements elsewhere — not out of loyalty to a dead commander, but out of a colder calculation: a hold that broke its word once could be expected to break it again, and nobody wanted to be next. The hold survived, technically, for another two generations, shrinking the entire time. Chronicle-Keepers studying the Unbroken Terms centuries later would point to this exact episode as the clearest proof available that the Terms had never been law in any enforceable sense — they were reputation wearing law's clothes, and reputation, it turned out, needed no army to collect on what it was owed.

### 6. The First Watching

The Antem hunters who spotted the newcomers along the northern coast did not report a threat. They reported a curiosity — small, wary encampments that broke and reformed every few days, never staying anywhere long enough to be worth the trouble of a formal approach. Weeks passed. Then months. The hunters began referring to the strangers, in the loose trade-pidgin that would eventually calcify into something closer to a real language, as "the driftwood people" — not a cruel name, by the standards of the people who coined it, just an accurate one. Driftwood didn't choose where it washed up. It didn't seem to want anything from the shore except to not be swept back out. Nobody on the Antem side considered these newcomers worth a formal delegation, a treaty, or even much curiosity beyond the kind a farmer gives an unfamiliar bird nesting in his field. That indifference, more than any hostility, was what let the earliest Faerl survive their first uncounted decades on Faerlan — not tolerated, exactly. Simply beneath notice, which turned out to be safer.

### 7. The White Wind

Renna Chases the Mirage had led refugee parties across worse ground than the Glass Plains before, and she said so, more than once, in the days leading up to the crossing — a boast that would follow her for the rest of her life in a form she never asked for. The wind came without warning on the second night, bone-white and utterly without smell, sound, or heat, which was somehow worse than if it had carried all three. Color left everything it touched — not darkness, but a specific, wrong kind of greyness, the color a memory goes when you've told the story too many times to trust your own recollection of it anymore. Three of her party were simply gone by morning, not dead in any sense anyone could point to, just absent, their bedrolls cold and undisturbed as if no one had ever slept in them. Renna herself walked out of the Glass Plains two days later, alive, unmarked, and changed in a way none of the survivors could quite name — not broken, not traumatized in any way that matched what they'd expected. Fearless, instead, in an unsettling register, as though whatever the wind had taken from her had also taken her capacity to be afraid of losing anything else. She never spoke of the three who vanished, not once, not even when directly asked by people who loved them. The idiom she left behind — "walked through the White Wind" — long since detached from her name, still describes exactly that specific, unnatural kind of changed: not resilience, not recovery, just a person who came back different and won't say how.

### 8. Landfall on a Borrowed Shore

They did not arrive as an army, or a migration, or even a plan. Faerling's worst Long Cold had split a losing faction off from the rest — the ones who couldn't win the argument over who stayed and who went, the ones who took what boats they could crew and simply left, trusting the water more than the people who'd have decided their fate otherwise. Generations passed aboard vessels that had never been built for a voyage this long, each one replacing the last, until nobody left alive on the boats had ever personally set foot on the shore they were supposedly returning to. What finally made landfall on Vaelume's coast was not conquest and was not even really arrival, in the sense the word usually carries — it was survivors of survivors, carrying almost nothing material, and one thing that mattered more than any cargo could have: a name, Faerl, inherited for a homeland none of them had ever actually seen, spoken with a conviction that had somehow outlasted every single person who could have explained where it came from. They built nothing grand in that first generation. They simply stayed, which by itself was more than the sea had ever let them do before.

### 9. The Warlord's Debt to No One

Dessin Holt ruled a stretch of contested Warlord Centuries territory the old-fashioned way — by refusing every offer of forge-debt, trade obligation, or marriage alliance that came with strings attached, paying for everything he needed in coin, labor, or blood, and owing nothing to anyone. It worked, for a while. His hold was smaller than his rivals' but genuinely his, unencumbered, answerable to no patron and beholden to no favor he'd have to eventually repay. He died wealthy, by the standards of his era, and completely unallied. Within a decade, his hold was absorbed piecemeal by three separate neighbors — not through conquest, but through the simple accumulation of small disputes that a debt-web of favors and obligations would ordinarily have resolved through arbitration, and which, in Dessin's case, had no framework to resolve them at all. Later chroniclers, working the story into a cautionary shape it may not entirely deserve, drew the lesson that independence and isolation, in this world, cost exactly the same price. Whether Dessin himself would have agreed with that reading is, appropriately, not recorded.

### 10. The Hollowing's Origin Trigger

There was a handler, Faerl, whose name did not survive the telling, tasked with guiding a small party of Antem travelers through ground her own people avoided by old, unspoken habit — not forbidden, exactly, just the kind of place nobody's grandmother had ever explained not to go, only insisted on. The travelers, for their own reasons, wanted to see what lay beneath the sealed earth everyone else stepped around. Whether the handler led them there believing it was safe, or warned them and was overruled, or simply didn't know enough to warn them at all, is a question no surviving account answers the same way twice — and the discrepancy is not an oversight in the record. It is the record. What's agreed on by every version is this: the ground gave way to something that had been sealed for longer than anyone's oral history reached back, and within a season, the first Antem communities downstream of that ground began showing symptoms nobody had a name for yet. Ilvane, a minor Antem chronicler working two valleys over, had written — months before any of it happened — an uneasy account of dreams about ground that shouldn't be disturbed, dismissed at the time as an old woman's nerves, preserved afterward as the closest thing this catastrophe has to a warning anyone actually heeded, and didn't. Whether the handler, the travelers, both, or neither actually bear responsibility for what followed is a question that every faction with a reason to answer it has answered differently, and none of those answers will ever be reconciled. That is not an accident of the record. It is the record's entire point.

### 11. The Last Light of House Verrin

The Verrin line was already fading before the Hollowing struck — a minor noble family with more pride than land, more debts than allies. Its patriarch scavenged a sealed, warm-without-fuel stone from ruin-ground during the plague's worst season and declared, with the specific conviction of a man who needs very badly to be right, that it proved his house had been chosen to survive what was killing everyone around them. He kept it burning at the center of his hall for three winters running, and for three winters, remarkably, he was right — his household stayed healthy while neighboring halls emptied one by one. On the fourth winter, without warning, without a single survivor to describe what happened, the hall and everyone in it — twelve people, servants included — were simply gone by morning. The stone floor had been glassed smooth, mirror-flat, and the walls had folded outward rather than collapsed inward, as though something inside had needed to leave rather than something outside had needed to get in. What survives of House Verrin today is a single word, still used by masons working that district for any floor melted too smooth and too evenly to have burned the ordinary way: verrin-glass. Nobody who uses the term remembers whose name it was originally attached to.

### 12. The Departure of the Stillwings

It did not happen gradually, though the sightings that preceded it had been thinning for nearly a decade. The bond between a Stillwing and its rider was, by every surviving account, something closer to kinship than to domestication — and as riders died in the plague's escalating waves, there was simply less holding each mount to any one place, less reason for a grief-bound creature to keep circling ground that no longer had anyone waiting on it. Then, on a single day that every surviving witness described identically despite having no way to have coordinated their accounts, every Stillwing left at once — not scattered, not gradual, a single visible departure that filled the sky and then simply didn't. Antem communities already reeling from plague and civil war took it as the worst omen any of them had personally witnessed, worse even than the disease itself, because disease could at least be blamed on something. The Stillwings' departure could only be read as a verdict. Where they went afterward has never been confirmed, and the handful of legends that claim an answer contradict each other enough that none has ever been treated as more than hopeful guesswork.

### 13. Fragmentation's Final Days

By the time the last scattered Antem clans understood there would be no recovery, there was no one left with the authority to declare it formally. The Rivening's civil wars had already burned through whatever centralized leadership survived the plague years, and what remained was clan-sized, then family-sized, then, in the final stretch, individuals — the last generation of a people who had built Antarkeia, and who died not in a single dramatic collapse but in the quiet, unglamorous way a fire goes out when nobody's left to feed it. The last Vessels died alongside them, in the same scattered, undramatic fashion — no battle, no final stand, just fewer and fewer each season until there were none. It was during this exact period, among the handful of Antem who understood clearly what was happening to their own people, that the doctrine later called the Proven Right first took shape — not as triumphant ideology, but as something closer to a suicide note dressed in the language of a settlement. Faerl who witnessed any part of this final stretch, and there were vanishingly few who did, carried away only fragments — a burned settlement here, an abandoned structure there — with no framework to understand what they were watching end. History simply closed the door on an entire people without anyone left alive who'd fully understood they were the last ones in the room.

### 14. The Hundred Years' Contest

Two Houses — never publicly named in surviving accounts as anything but "the mountain claimant" and "the river claimant," a discretion later scholars suspect was deliberate — fought three wars, generations apart, over a single mountain pass whose strategic value had stopped mattering to either side by the second war and had become pure inherited grievance by the third. The first war was fought over genuine trade access. The second, a full generation later, was fought because the first war's peace terms had been worded ambiguously enough that both sides could plausibly claim the other had broken them. The third war, fought by descendants who had never personally seen the pass in question and inherited the conflict the way one inherits a family feud rather than a strategic asset, ended not in victory for either side but in mutual exhaustion — both Houses simply stopped sending soldiers, without ever formally agreeing to. By the third war's quiet end, neither side's chroniclers could articulate, without consulting older records, what specifically their House had originally been fighting for. The pass itself remains, to this day, contested in name only — nobody's crossed it in force for longer than either House's living memory extends.

### 15. The Second Crossing

Generations after Corwin Ashe returned alone from the doomed Charter of Salt and Silence expedition, a smaller, quieter coastal proto-house — with no central Crown left standing to forbid the attempt — tried again. They rigged a lighter vessel, crewed it with sailors who'd grown up on Ashe's half-told stories rather than dismissing them, and reached the Vantry Isles without losing a single crew member. What they found wasn't conquest, or trade, or even much mystery: people. Islanders whose oral fragments about "ships that fell from the sky" lined up, close enough to be unsettling, with Faerlan's own half-forgotten origin stories — not identical, filtered too many generations through both sides' own myth-drift for that, but close enough that both crews walked away from the meeting privately convinced, without either side ever using the word aloud, that they were looking at distant kin. Nothing was formally recorded, nothing proven, and the crossing wasn't repeated for generations afterward — but the quiet certainty it left behind on both sides outlasted every detail of the actual voyage.

### 16. Verah Kestrel and the Founding of the Gathering Crown

She was offered, before she'd taken a single hold by force, marriage into an already-dominant clan that would have handed her authority without a fight — and she refused it, publicly enough that the refusal itself became the seed of every story told about her afterward, whether or not it happened exactly the way the songs eventually shaped it. What Verah Kestrel actually built, over the following decades, was not a conquest but an argument made real: that the scattered warlord territories sprawling across the continent since the Warlord Centuries could be bound together not through domination but through something more durable — a shared script, a shared ledger, roads that meant something the same way in every territory they crossed. She fought when she had to. She married when it served the same purpose war would have, at less cost. By the time she died, the Gathering Crown spanned more territory, administered more evenly, than anything Faerlan had produced before or would produce again for a thousand years. She left behind no instructions for what happened after her, trusting — wrongly, as it turned out — that the institutions she'd built were sturdier than any one heir's competence.

### 17. The Writ of Tamrin

Tamrin Kestrel inherited a realm he could not, by any honest accounting, actually hold. He was not a weak ruler by temperament — those who knew him described someone genuinely trying to govern a Crown that had simply outgrown any one person's capacity to administer it directly. The regional governors did not move against him one at a time, the way a conventional rebellion might have. They moved together, all nine at once, in a coordinated demand that stripped central enforcement power from the throne in exchange for letting Tamrin keep the crown in name. He signed. There was no battle, no dramatic last stand — just a document, and a young king who understood, correctly, that refusing to sign would have cost him everything the signing merely cost him half of. History has been kinder to the Writ of Tamrin than his own contemporaries were. It's remembered now less as a surrender than as the moment "provincial rights" became something citable, arguable, real — a legal concept every later House, including three that didn't yet exist when Tamrin signed, would eventually trace its own territorial claim back to, whether they knew his name or not.

### 18. Varic Sennor and the Last Crown

He came closer than anyone since Verah Kestrel herself to reforging what the Writ of Tamrin had let slip away — conquering back, in barely fifteen years, most of the fractured provinces that eight hundred years of drift had let go their separate ways. Varic Sennor was not a gentle conqueror. He was efficient in a way that left little room for the kind of institution-building Kestrel had favored, trusting instead that speed and momentum would hold things together until he had time to formalize what he'd won by force. He never got that time. A wound that should not have killed him — infected, slow, entirely survivable by any ordinary reckoning — took him within days of what would prove to be his final victory, with no named heir and no clear successor among his own commanders. His four surviving regional commanders, each already holding the ground his campaigns had won, faced a choice: fight each other for the whole prize, or divide it along the lines they already controlled and call it settled. They chose the second option, not out of trust in one another, but out of exhaustion with fifteen years of someone else's war. Those four divisions became House Dray, House Colehurst, House Thorne, and House Marlow — not a slow administrative hardening, as an earlier account of this era mistakenly assumed, but the direct, immediate consequence of one man's near-success and his unlucky, unglamorous death.

### 19. The Withering

It began in a single river-valley community, the way most catastrophes in this world begin: quietly, with no one recognizing what they were looking at until it was already too late to contain. A relic long buried beneath the valley's oldest settlement cracked — from age, from weather, from nothing anyone could later identify — and released something that spread through the population as a slow, grey-tinged wasting sickness, unmistakably reminiscent of the despair-coded phenomena from the Genesis crash, though not a single person alive to suffer it had any framework connecting the two. Entire manor populations died down to a fraction of their former size within two generations. The old feudal labor bonds that had, however imperfectly, held the Long Fracture's remaining provincial structures together simply had no one left to enforce them — survivors, Faerl and Farhand alike, found themselves working the same depleted land side by side out of pure necessity, negotiating terms with each other for the first time instead of simply obeying whoever held the deed. The Reckoning Statutes, drafted generations later to formalize Farhand inheritance rights, trace their actual root not to any single act of compassion but to this exact labor shortage — a wound nobility never fully recovered enough leverage to close back up.

### 20. The Fall of Waelbrook

Nobody in Waelbrook knew they were living directly above a dormant spoke-site — nobody in the wider world knew either, since the town had been settled generations after Antarkeia's containment network had already gone quiet and unremarkable, indistinguishable from any other patch of ground. When it failed, it failed completely and without warning, not the slow leak every other affected region across the continent had been dealing with for centuries, but total, instant collapse. The town was emptied in a single night — worse, by every surviving account, than anything either bordering House had inflicted on the other across generations of low-grade war. For the first time in longer than either House's chroniclers could reliably date, Colehurst and Thorne responded to a shared threat together instead of each quietly hoping the other's losses would be worse. It wasn't trust. It was the specific, cold recognition that whatever had happened to Waelbrook could happen to either of them next, and that the old rivalry suddenly looked much smaller than it had the week before. That alliance — born from one border town's total erasure — became the actual seed of the four-House coalition that would, a century later, finally march on Antarkeia itself.

### 21. Ford at Wrenfield

Kester Dray had crossed that particular ford a dozen times before without incident, which was precisely why nobody thought to warn him the thirteenth time would be different. His horse stumbled — on what, exactly, wasn't clear until after the fact, when the buried Antem-era fragment his mount's hoof had struck was finally excavated and identified. He was thrown, and the fall itself, an ordinary fall by any measure, proved fatal in the specific, unlucky way that ordinary falls occasionally are. Halric Colcross, traveling with him under the Shared Roof's fostering tradition, was the one who carried the news back — and was, in the confused hours immediately afterward, wrongly blamed by commoners who didn't recognize either man on sight and assumed the foster-brother must have had something to gain. The court, which knew the fostering arrangement intimately, never seriously entertained the accusation. What lingers about Kester Dray's death isn't mystery — the causal chain is as clean as anything in Dray's genealogy gets — it's the specific, almost insulting ordinariness of it: a paramount lord, killed not by war, rival, or demon, but by a half-buried scrap of a civilization five thousand years extinct, still capable of one last, indifferent act of violence.

### 22. Ythrin the Unspent

He walked the deepest galleries of Antarkeia for all eleven days of the Breach without retreating once, without serious injury, present at nearly every major turning point the other nine vignette-figures individually witnessed only pieces of. Then, the moment the innermost seal finally broke, Ythrin simply walked away — not fled, not collapsed, walked, with the same unhurried scout's pace he'd kept the entire time — and took none of the spoils everyone around him was already beginning to gather. Faerl accounts written soon after called it cowardice, the obvious reading for anyone who hadn't been down in the deep galleries with him and didn't understand what eleven days of that particular pressure actually does to a person. A quieter, later tradition offers a different reading: that Ythrin had simply seen enough, by day eleven, to understand exactly what kind of victory this was, and wanted no part of profiting from it. Both readings survive in the record, side by side, neither one ever fully displacing the other — which is, on reflection, probably the most honest way this particular story could have been left.

### 23. The Boneyard Reckoning

For the first thirty years after the Breach, demons were handled the way they'd always been handled in legend, which is to say badly and locally — whoever happened to be nearby when one surfaced, usually a hedge-warrior or a village simply abandoning the affected ground and hoping the thing stayed put. That changed under the petty-king Ossian Vale, after a single summer in which four separate holds along his border were emptied faster than any army could realistically respond. Vale's solution wasn't glory-seeking, and it wasn't even particularly brave — it was administrative. He formalized what had been ad hoc into a standing response: trained companies, funded through a new tithe, tasked specifically with responding to demon incursions before they could spread rather than after. The tithe was resented from its first collection, and the earliest Boneyard companies were, by most accounts, undertrained and underequipped for what they were being asked to do. But the framework held, and it's still, in a much-evolved form, the closest thing the present era has to organized demon response — a bureaucratic solution to an apocalyptic problem, which is either the most Faerl thing imaginable or the only thing that could have possibly worked.

### 24. The Smoke Year War and the Forgehand Rising

Aldous Colehurst did not set out to destroy his own House. He set out, by every surviving account of his early reign, to secure it — tightening conscription, raising forge-tithes on vassal holds that had grown comfortable under looser terms, all justified as necessary preparation against threats that, to be fair to him, were genuinely increasing across the whole continent. Three vassal houses broke rather than comply. The war that followed burned through Fire-zone territory for eight years, and Colehurst's own capital, the seat of a Dominion that had briefly held more land than any House since Consolidation, fell not to any of the rebelling vassals but to an uprising led from inside its own walls by Garrick Forgehand, a low-born forge-hand who'd spent the war years watching conscription quotas hollow out his own district and decided, correctly as it turned out, that the House's grip on power was weaker than it looked. The Rising took his name directly. Colehurst survived the war as a House, diminished, its territory a fraction of the Dominion's peak — and the Concord of the Four Hearths, convened the following year specifically to prevent any other paramount House unraveling this completely in isolation, exists entirely because of what happened when this one did.

### 25. The Concord of the Four Hearths

The summit convened on Thorne ground, deliberately neutral, barely a year after Colehurst's Dominion had finished collapsing in on itself — and every delegate in the room understood, without needing it said aloud, that they were there because it could just as easily have been any of their Houses instead. What emerged wasn't an alliance in any formal military sense. It was something quieter and, in the end, more durable: the Shared Roof, a formalized cross-House fostering tradition meant to ensure that no future paramount heir would grow up entirely ignorant of the other three Houses' internal workings, administered primarily by Thorne, whose neutral territory had hosted the summit in the first place. It was not universally welcomed. Several vassal lords on all four sides privately resented what amounted to sending their heirs to be half-raised by potential rivals. But it held, generation after generation, quietly producing exactly the kind of cross-House trust its architects had hoped for — including, decades later, the specific fostering bond between Kester Dray and Halric Colcross that would put an innocent man briefly, wrongly, under suspicion at a river ford neither of them could have known would matter.
