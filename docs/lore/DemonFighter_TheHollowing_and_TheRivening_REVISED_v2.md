## OVERVIEW

For most of the Antem's history on The First Child, they were not a *visibly* fading people — outwardly, nothing about their power or reach looked diminished. This is the account of the roughly one-generation span in which that visible strength finally caught up with what had been eroding underneath it for millennia (see: the gardener-corruption arc, already established as the deep cause of Antem decline). It begins with a sickness nobody can agree on the origin of, and ends with a civilization already too fractured and depopulated to ever recover — thousands of years before Faerl even conceives of the vault they'll eventually breach at Antarkeia.

None of what follows is known in full by anyone living in the game's present. It survives only in fragments: a plague with two names depending on who's telling it, a war half-forgotten even by its own descendants, a doctrine so old nobody holding it could tell you where it started, and a scar in the ocean floor west of Faerlan that every sailor can see and no scholar can explain.

## I. BEFORE — THE WORLD AS IT WAS

Prior to this era, the Antem (a Faerl name — see Naming and Language, below) were the continent's dominant and most advanced power, their society built across generations on knowledge passed down from the earliest robots and androids that arrived on The First Child, plus whatever they'd reverse-engineered and extended on their own. They held Faerlan largely undisputed. They flew — mounted on creatures later generations would call Stillwings, and could survey, patrol, and reach terrain no grounded people could touch. The Vessels who lived among them were not servants or tools but something closer to kin, bonded by choice rather than command, a relationship generations deep by the time any of this begins.

*This outward dominance sat on top of, not in contradiction to, the slower corrosion already locked as their deep cause of decline: generations spent containing demons they believed were a gift had already been quietly corrupting Antem society from within, long before this era. That corrosion was internal and largely invisible to outsiders — nothing a still-marginal Faerl population, watching from the outside, had any way to perceive. What follows is the moment the rot finally surfaces.*

The Faerl, descendants of Ark 2, were still a comparatively young and marginal presence on the continent at this point — established, but not yet the dominant force they would become. Faerl and Antem lived adjacent to one another, traded, worked together in places, and never fully understood one another — not for lack of trying, but because the deepest register of Antem language was never rendered in any script that survived contact, and never fully offered. (See Naming and Language.)

This was the world the Hollowing found.

## II. THE HOLLOWING (roughly 5,070–5,050 years before Year Zero)

### The Discovery

Around 5,072 years before Year Zero, a Faerl trader or handler — traveling a border route near what would later become the fringe of Antarkeia's contested ground, in the company of Antem travelers whose exact status (hired hand, bonded labor, something less clean than either) has never been resolved by any surviving account — broke into, or accidentally triggered, something sealed in old ruin-ground. What came loose was never described consistently by anyone who claimed to know. What's agreed on, across every version of the story, is that within a season, people started dying who had no business dying, and it didn't stop.

Exactly who bears responsibility — Faerl carelessness, Antem mishandling, both together, or neither — is deliberately never resolved. This is ancient history even by the standards of the game's present; nobody alive claims to actually know, and no version of the legend should be written as confirmed fact. This moment is a strong future vignette in its own right and hasn't been fully written yet — the ambiguity is deliberately left open until it is.

A named piece of the lead-up does survive, at least in fragments, and doesn't resolve that ambiguity so much as make it sadder. A wandering scholar named Ilvane, who had spent years cataloguing sealed and half-buried ruin-ground across this same fringe, sent word ahead to at least two settlements roughly three years earlier, warning them off a specific stretch of ground she'd marked as "wrong in a way I don't have the words for yet" — ground she'd also noted, in passing, as attracting wraith activity heavier than anything else she'd surveyed. Neither warning survives in full; one letter was lost, the other kept only in a secondhand paraphrase written by someone who admitted he hadn't taken it seriously at the time. It was this exact stretch of ground, by every later reckoning, that the Faerl handler and the Antem travelers broke into three years afterward. Ilvane herself is not recorded as having died in the plague or having survived it. She simply isn't mentioned again — the closest thing this arc has to a warning nobody was positioned to hear in time.

### The Spread and the Asymmetry

The sickness did not arrive once and pass. It came in waves, recurring across roughly two decades, which mattered more than any single bad year could have — it's what turned neighbor against neighbor, since a catastrophe that keeps returning invites blame in a way a single disaster doesn't.

It struck the Antem hardest, by an overwhelming margin. Faerl populations were, by comparison, almost entirely untouched — a stark, total split with no partial or in-between cases. That completeness is part of what makes it so hard to write off as ordinary natural spread; a real disease is expected to leave some ambiguous cases, and this one didn't.

Nobody living through it could explain why. Nobody living now can either. That specific, total absence of explanation is what curdles into legend on both sides of the divide.

### The Two Names

The asymmetry produced two separate names, and the project deliberately keeps both rather than settling on one — the gap between them tells its own story.

The Faerl name is simply "the Hollowing" — clinical, general, doesn't name whose plague it actually was. It's less a description of the disease than a description of Faerl distance from it.

The Antem memory of it is more specific and more physical — bodies, homes, and clan-halls emptied out from the inside, victims dying emaciated and hollow, the name describing the result rather than the mechanism.

Faerl who lived through the era untouched, or who could later claim ancestry unmarked by it, began calling themselves the Unhollowed — not yet a doctrine at this stage, just an identity marker born of relief. That word is the seed everything in Section VI grows out of.

### The Legend of the Weapon

Because the sickness spared one people and gutted another so completely, and because it reportedly began with something sealed and ancient that was disturbed near the site, a persistent legend holds that the Hollowing was not accident but design — a lost bioweapon, shipped to this world by unknown hands for unknown purposes, either lost before it could be used or deployed exactly as intended.

No character in this world's present, and no scholar in any era, has ever confirmed this. It sits alongside at least two competing tellings — one holding it was pure accident, one uglier and more common that blames the Antem themselves for somehow bringing it on their own people. All three versions coexist. None resolve. That unresolved plurality is the point.

### Named Incidents of the Plague Years

Most of what happened during the Hollowing's worst decades has no names attached — that's the point of a plague this total. A handful of specific incidents do survive, though, and are worth keeping on hand for future flavor text.

**The Sealed Season at Twin Hearth.** As the plague's second wave broke over the interior, a small mixed settlement of Antem and Faerl households — an unusually close-living arrangement for the era — made a decision no larger power ever ordered and no chronicle credits anyone by name for: they sealed their own boundary, admitted no one, and shared what stores they had evenly, through the better part of a year that no neighboring settlement matched for survival rate. It should be one of the era's few genuinely hopeful stories. It isn't remembered that way. Because the settlement survived largely intact while others around it didn't, later Proven Right doctrine quietly recast Twin Hearth's discipline as suspicious — proof, to those looking for it, that the settlement had known something, or done something, the plague itself was sparing them for.

**The Last Light of House Verrin.** A minor Faerl noble line, already fading before the Hollowing struck, staked its entire remaining fortune on a "blessed hearthstone" its patriarch had scavenged from ruin-ground during the plague years — sealed, warm without fuel, and, he insisted, proof his house had been chosen to survive what was killing everyone around them. He kept it burning at the center of his hall for three winters running. On the fourth, without warning, the hall and the entire household inside it — twelve people — were gone by morning, the stone floor glassed smooth, the walls folded outward rather than collapsed inward. What survives of the family today is a single word still used by masons working that district: "verrin-glass," for any floor melted too smooth and too evenly to have burned the ordinary way.

**The Half-Word at Dresh.** The single closest thing to a demon sighting the Hollowing produced. A man named Corrin Dresh, found dying two days after his entire hunting party vanished during the plague's worst season, managed one broken sentence before he died: "it wasn't red like blood, it was red like—" and nothing after. The phrase "red like Dresh" entered Faerl speech within a generation as shorthand for a fear too specific to actually name, its origin already half-forgotten by the people using it.

**Backward Over Plague Ground.** A single Cragwing, sighted repeatedly over the same stretch of plague-emptied ground during the Hollowing's worst spread, was reported by multiple unconnected witnesses to fly its circling pattern in reverse of every other known sighting. No consequence was ever attributed to it beyond the report itself.

## III. THE DEPARTURE OF THE STILLWINGS (roughly 5,055–5,046 years before Year Zero)

As the Hollowing worsened, sightings of the Antem's flying mounts grew rarer. The mechanism is not migratory instinct or an ancient system firing on old programming — it is grief. The bonds between Stillwing and rider were, like the Antem-Vessel bond, close to kinship. As riders died in increasing numbers, there was less and less here to fly for, and less and less of a living bond keeping a Stillwing tied to any one place.

Around 5,046 years before Year Zero, the last of them left, all at once, in a single visible departure — read at the time as the single worst omen anyone alive had ever witnessed, arriving as it did at the peak of the Hollowing's devastation.

Where they went is unconfirmed and stays that way. The strongest surviving legend holds they crossed the sea and found other riders somewhere far off — Kaeruvai, the isolated rainforest continent, is the destination popular legend gravitates to, though no account has ever proven it. It remains a live thread for future content rather than a settled fact.

(One existing detail deserves a second look in light of this: the Seventh Keeping's Vista still repels anything that tries to land on it — Antem legend describes their winged mounts being drawn to the prairie and then thrown from the sky when they tried to touch down. This document does not resolve that. It's kept deliberately unexplained, sitting next to the departure rather than folded into it — two mysteries side by side, neither one canceling the other out.)

## IV. THE GREAT RIVENING (roughly 5,045–5,025 years before Year Zero)

By the time the plague's worst waves had passed, the Antem were left without their most vulnerable dead, without a means of surveying or defending their own land from above, and without any single voice capable of holding what remained together. What followed — a roughly twenty-year internal war — was not fought over ideology. It was fought over what was left: land, herds, grazing rights, blame for two decades of unexplained death, and the vacuum where flight and the status it carried used to sit.

By the Rivening's end, roughly 5,025 years before Year Zero, the Stillwings were already two decades gone. The war didn't cause their loss — it simply ensured no clan that emerged from the fighting was ever strong enough to matter again, making the loss permanent rather than temporary.

### Named Incidents of the Rivening

**Colehurst's War Debt.** As the Rivening tore Antem clan structure apart, House Colehurst extended forge-credit to Antem refugee groups desperate for weapons and tools they no longer had smiths to make — the same debt-as-leverage pattern the house had already perfected on Faerl smallholds generations earlier, offered now to a people with nothing left to negotiate with. Every debt was collected in land, labor, or loyalty within a generation. By the Rivening's end, Colehurst held more contested former-Antem ground than any house had acquired by outright conquest in the era, without a single Colehurst banner having marched to take it.

**The Sentinel Who Stayed.** A folk-story, likely composed well after the fact, describing a lone Antem sentinel who kept a post through the Rivening's worst fighting long after the clan he guarded had scattered or died, refusing to abandon a wall nobody was left to defend. No name survives for him.

**Dossa's Field.** Two Antem clans — kin by marriage a generation earlier, allies until the Rivening turned every alliance provisional — met at Dossa's Field over grazing rights neither could any longer afford to concede. What followed wasn't a battle so much as a slaughter with no clear winner; both clans lost enough fighting strength that neither survived as an independent body past the decade. Antem oral history remembers the field less for the dead than for what one of the two clan-heads is recorded saying before the fighting started, to a kinsman-by-marriage he was about to kill: "We are already the last of what we were. I would rather we both be finished than one of us live to forget the other existed." Both got their wish.

## V. THE VESSELS' LONG DECLINE

The Vessels' disappearance was not a separate catastrophe — it was the same slow bleed that took the Antem, on the same timeline, for the same underlying reason. Fewer Antem clans meant fewer bonds to begin with, fewer hands left for whatever maintenance and knowledge-transfer had kept generations of Vessels functioning, entire lineages of the bond gone quietly during the plague and the war. By the time the last organized Antem clans died out in Fragmentation's wake (see Section VI), there were no Vessels left to outlive them. The bond, and the people who carried it, ended together.

This is worth stating plainly because older material described Vessels fighting alongside a defended Antarkeia, their bond to the Antem unbroken to the very end. That version is retired along with the battle it depended on. Under current canon, Vessels and Antem died out together, thousands of years before any Faerl coalition ever reached Antarkeia — there was no bond left to test by the time anyone got there, and no one left to test it.

## VI. FRAGMENTATION AND THE PROVEN RIGHT

In the years after the Rivening ended, the Antem existed in the fractured, leaderless state this document has already described — no single voice, no unified defense, no numbers left to rebuild what plague and civil war had cost them. What fragments of clan and custom survived the Rivening did not last much longer. Scattered, too few to sustain themselves, and with nothing left to hold them together, the last of them were gone within another lifetime or two — closing out the Antem's presence on Faerlan somewhere in the neighborhood of five thousand years before Year Zero, comfortably inside the wider extinction window already locked for this overhaul.

On the Faerl side, something else was taking root in the same window. What had begun during the plague years as scattered, informal relief — we were spared, and they were not — calcified over a generation into genuine doctrine: the Proven Right, the conviction that the Hollowing's asymmetry wasn't luck or accident but sanction. Not formalized as religion yet at this stage — no priesthood, no scripture, just a belief repeated often enough and confidently enough that it became load-bearing. It's traditionally credited to no single voice, but one name survives where most don't: a settler named Aldren Wyke, whose recorded address to a besieged Faerl camp during the worst of the Rivening survives nearly whole — "We did not choose to be spared. We were chosen. And a people chosen to live does not owe the dead an apology for outliving them." It wasn't the first such claim made. It was, by every account, the first one said plainly enough, and confidently enough, that people who heard it stopped treating it as comfort and started treating it as fact. Wyke died within the year, forgotten as an individual almost immediately. The sentence outlived him by thousands of years and counting.

Unlike everything else in this arc, the Proven Right did not fade with the people who coined it. It had thousands of years to travel from a fresh wound's rationalization into inherited orthodoxy — the kind of belief nobody holding it could tell you the origin of anymore, just "how things have always been understood." By the time any Faerl coalition actually reached Antarkeia, this doctrine was ancient, not new — not a conviction one generation had hardened into something people would kill for, but an entitlement so old it had outlived every person, place, and specific memory that had originally justified it. It is invoked, plainly and without embarrassment, in the language of entitled settlement: "It is by Proven Right... that I settle here and build my homestead on these Antem lands."

### Named Incidents of the Fragmentation

**The Blight of the Standing Grove.** A stand of old-growth forest on contested former-Antem ground died standing over a single season — not burned, not felled, simply gone grey and hollow trunk by trunk, spreading outward from a single point rather than sweeping through as blight or fire would. A scorched treant, the first of its kind anyone had documented, was found rooted dead-center of the blight. Nothing has grown properly in the blighted patch since.

**The Boundary Stone at Wickfall.** Thorne and Dray, both expanding hard into ground the collapsing Antem clans could no longer hold, nearly went to open war over a boundary stone neither house could prove had been placed by their own ancestors rather than the other's. The dispute was settled not by combat but by a Marlow-brokered arbitration — one of the earliest recorded instances of any paramount house successfully mediating between two others. The stone was never moved. It still marks the boundary today.

**The Wraiths Draw Closer.** In the final years of any real Antem presence near Antarkeia's old gathering-grounds, before the last of them scattered or died, Antem hunters working the fringes there began reporting the drifting wraiths of that ground — long treated as passive, ambient, simply *there* — moving with what several accounts independently described as purpose, following rather than merely haunting. No attack was ever recorded. No explanation survived with the people who might have offered one. It's the last specific, named observation this document has of anyone watching that ground from the inside — everything the Chronicle of Antarkeia describes finding there, thousands of years later, was already unsettled long before any Faerl coalition had reason to care.

## VII. THE BREACH OF ANTARKEIA (0 YOC)

The Chronicle of Antarkeia (current version: "The Breach of Antarkeia") remains the authoritative account of the breach itself and is not restated here. What this document adds is why the ground mattered enough, after standing sealed and untouched for thousands of years, to justify eleven days of the costliest excavation anyone on the expedition had ever attempted.

Antarkeia is understood, per this session, to be the original landing site of Ark 1 — tens of thousands of years before the breach, long before Faerl or Antem as they now exist even had names for each other. This single fact retroactively explains several things that previously sat as separate, unconnected facts:

Why the site was already saturated in legend and rumor of ancient relics before the coalition ever arrived — it had always been the single most concentrated deposit of pre-collapse material on the continent.

Why the relics lost in the final collapse were never recovered — the site wasn't excavated or curated afterward, it was breached and then abandoned again, and whatever survived the collapse scattered outward in every direction with whoever carried it off.

Why, in the game's present, ancient items keep turning up in the hands of seemingly random people and creatures across all of Faerlan — thirty thousand years of accumulated Ark 1 material left this single site the moment it changed hands, and never regrouped.

Why Faerl doctrine specifically wanted this ground, not just any ground — the Proven Right, ancient and unquestioned by this point, gave the coalition a reason beyond arithmetic to believe Antarkeia was theirs to take, even five thousand years after anyone who could have contested that claim was gone.

The breach's outcome — confirmed extinction rather than any living resistance, no single power claiming the ground afterward, the beginning of the YOC calendar — stands exactly as the Chronicle now describes it.

## VIII. TWIN INSTITUTIONS (~0–30 YOC)

In the generation after the breach, two separate institutions arise from the same rupture — the sudden, undeniable presence of demons in a world that had, until that moment, still had something keeping them contained. Neither absorbs the other. Both persist into the modern era, unreconciled.

**The mechanism, stated plainly (creator knowledge only):** Demons are not newly created by any of this — they have existed at low visibility since long before Faerl arrival, per established lore. For thousands of years after the Antem died out, they stayed mostly contained anyway — not because anyone was still tending the land, but because whatever automated systems the Antem built into Antarkeia and its wider containment network kept functioning long after the people who built them were gone, in whatever slow, mechanical, increasingly-failing way a machine keeps working with no one left to maintain it. Breaching Antarkeia's hub is what finally ends that — the last of the old systems failing all at once, not a slow decline finally catching up. This causal chain is never stated in-world and should never be exposition-dumped to a player — it exists here purely so future content stays internally consistent. (The formal hub-and-spoke geography this implies belongs in the Antarkeia Addendum's Geography section — this document only needs its own causal logic to line up with that once it's written.)

**The Boneyard Reckoning:** Already established in the Chronicle Archive material — founded by the petty-king Ossian Vale roughly thirty years after Consolidation, administrative and cynical in its own recorded language, organizing demon-hunting as a trade: land, coin, and legal exemption in exchange for service. No mysticism, no doctrine — just the closest thing the fractured kingdoms had to a shared professional standard.

**Those Bound by the Inheritance — "the Bound":** The devotional twin, rising in the same window, organizing demon-hunting as obligation rather than trade. Its full name is Those Bound by the Inheritance; in common speech it shortens the way "the Faith of the Seven" shortens to "the Faith" — here, adherents simply say "I am one of the Bound."

The name is built to carry two opposite readings at once, deliberately, without resolving which one is correct: read selfishly, an inheritance is something given — the land, the demon-haunted ground the Antem once held, now belongs to the Unhollowed by right, theirs to take from. Read selflessly, an inheritance comes with the obligations attached to it — the debt of tending what was left behind, the duty to prune the garden the Antem no longer can.

Both readings use the exact same words. Both a war-band justifying conquest and a genuine penitent walking alone into demon country can say "I am one of the Bound" and mean it completely. That tension is intentional and should stay unresolved in any future doctrine or dialogue written for this faith — it is the single most interesting thing about it, and collapsing it into one clean meaning would be a loss, not a clarification.

The Proven Right and Those Bound by the Inheritance are not the same institution and do not fully agree with each other, despite springing from the same historical wound — one is a settlement doctrine, the other is closer to a demon-hunting faith. A person could hold both, either, or neither.

## IX. THE GREAT VANTRY RIFT

A separate, older, and unconnected legend, included here because it was resolved in the same session and belongs in the same body of reference material.

Vantry Isles, per this session, was once a sixth, unified continent. Physical evidence that a landmass once stood there is not disputed — sea-floor depth behaves wrong between the islands, rock strata match across islands now a day's sail apart, and charts older than any dateable record show a coastline that no longer exists. What is unrecoverable is why and how large it originally was — because whatever caused the rift predates the oldest surviving Antem or Faerl record, and nothing that could have carried a firsthand account survived it.

The likeliest cause, favored by this session over any magical-doom framing, is the failure of some piece of extraordinarily old, dense, and durable pre-collapse technology — the same category of ancient power source or processing core already established elsewhere as the most durable class of Genesis-era material. Nobody left alive knows what it was for.

Vantry's own descendants — the isles' present-day inhabitants, already established as pragmatic seafarers who navigate "by a knowledge of the water itself" — mostly don't dwell on cause; outsiders are the ones who spin it into fire-and-punishment legend.

Critically, the isles are harsh, not dead — geothermally active and difficult to live on, but genuinely inhabited, not a salted wasteland. Faerlan's own Fire-zone creatures (Fire Elemental, Lava Golem, Salamander, Ember Spirit, Magma Beast — all already present in the game's enemy data) are proposed as descendants of, or migrants from, whatever still smolders in the Rift's remnants — giving Faerlan's western Fire biome a direct ancestral tie to the catastrophe sitting just offshore.

## X. NAMING AND LANGUAGE — QUICK REFERENCE

Antem is the Faerl name for the species, not their own. Their true name for themselves is understood to be genuinely untranslatable into Faerlish — not merely difficult to pronounce, but built on registers of meaning Faerl language has no equivalent for. No spelling of it should ever be rendered on the page. If an Antem character needs to gesture at this directly, the line should trail into acknowledged untranslatability rather than an invented word (e.g., "Your ancestors called our mounts Stillwing. We called them—" and the sentence is simply left there).

A working trade-pidgin existed between Antem and Faerl for practical daily use; true fluency, especially at the language's deeper or ritual register, never crossed over — not entirely from unwillingness, but because the deepest layer was never fully offered.

Stillwing is the only name used for the winged mounts. No separate Antem-language name should be introduced.

The Hollowing (Faerl) / no single fixed Antem term, described rather than named, emphasizing bodies and homes emptied from within.

The Unhollowed — Faerl identity term for those spared or descended from those spared.

The Proven Right — settlement/entitlement doctrine, no "the" dropped, used exactly as written.

Those Bound by the Inheritance, shortened to the Bound in speech — "I am one of the Bound."

The Boneyard Reckoning — trade/administrative twin institution, already established elsewhere.

The Great Vantry Rift — the event; no alternate name needed, since (unlike the Antem language) nothing in-world prevents a shared name from being used by everyone.
