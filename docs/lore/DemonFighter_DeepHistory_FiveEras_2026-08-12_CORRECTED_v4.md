## 0. THE FAERL ORIGIN STORY (new — fills a real gap in prior lore)

**Scope note, resolving the Species Canon conflict flagged in the last audit:** Ark 2 was a deportation vessel from a faction that didn't care where it landed (established in `Reference.docx`), and it scattered on arrival — small, disconnected pockets across multiple landmasses, avoiding Faerlan mostly by indifference rather than design, since Faerlan was already the one continent with an established Antem/Vessel civilization worth steering clear of. That also explains, tidily, why Ark 1 landed there and Ark 2 didn't: Antarkeia was the good, deliberate landing; Ark 2 was the leftover nobody bothered aiming. Species Canon's "sent to Faerlan" isn't wrong — Faerlan is where the largest surviving branch eventually converged, generations later, which is functionally the same as "sent" from the perspective of everyone who's lived there since. What follows below is **the specific branch this document follows** — the Ossmoor pocket — not a claim that this is where every Ark 2 survivor ended up. Other pockets on Kessara, Vantry, or Kaeruvai remain unconfirmed, unexplored hooks for a future session.

This branch came down on **Ossmoor**, the cold northern continent already locked in your geography — isolated and harsh enough that a small, cryo-frozen-mindset crew with minimal equipment could survive there for millennia without ever being noticed by anyone on Faerlan. Over the Great Forgetting and the Deep Dream era, the exiles built a marginal coastal foothold they came to call **Faerling** — not a nation, just the name of the strip of coast that kept them alive. "Faerl" is the ethnonym that outlived the place-name, the way real peoples end up named for a homeland nobody living has seen.

What actually pushed them south was a recurring climate cycle Ossmoor settlers came to call **the Long Cold** — a punishing stretch of hard winters roughly every few centuries that squeezed an already marginal coastline past what it could support. The decisive cycle (placed here at **BYT 1000**) split Faerling into rival factions fighting over what little arable land remained — the first real Faerl infighting on record, and the direct ancestor pattern the four paramount houses would later repeat at a much larger scale. The losing faction took to boats and drifted south along the coast, generation replacing generation aboard vessels never meant for a voyage this long. What finally made landfall on Faerlan's northern shore was not an invading force — it was survivors of survivors, carrying nothing but each other and a name for a homeland none of them had personally seen.

**What Faerlan was called before Faerl arrived:** the Antem were never one polity long enough to need a single name for the whole continent — the Nine Hearths came closest, and it dissolved on its own (see §2). But one old name survives, poetic rather than political: in the oldest surviving Long Grief liturgy, the whole land is called **Vaelume — "the Unforgetting Ground."** It's the one word every Antem clan tradition seems to independently agree on, even across clans with no contact with one another. **"Faerlan" as the name of the entire continent is itself a Faerl imposition** — nobody had bothered naming the whole landmass as one thing before, because only a people trying to claim it as one thing would need to.

## THE GREAT FORGETTING — one marker

**Unmarked Time — The Long Drift** *[stub — History]*
Somewhere in the unrecorded span between Ark 2's landing and the oldest attested Antem memory, the exiles who would become the Faerl did not vanish — they simply stopped mattering to anyone keeping a record. Scattered, few, and without the tools their ancestors had once had, they survived in small pockets far from what would become Antem-dominated ground, leaving nothing behind that any later chronicler could date. When Faerl finally reappear in any Antem account, millennia later, no one on either side has any memory that this is a reunion rather than a first meeting.

## 2. THE DEEP DREAM — BYT 5800 to BYT 2100
*Antem-only. No Faerl presence. 13 events — 8 MAJOR, 5 stub.*

**BYT 5820 — The Long Hunger** *[MAJOR — Drought/Famine/Creature]*
Before there was a faith called the Long Grief, there was a decade of green things simply failing — wells running backward from every season anyone trusted, herds calving stillborn two years running. The clan of Meluen, largest on the eastern grass, lost enough young that it never again fielded its old numbers. What made the famine unforgettable wasn't only the hunger, but what the hunger drove down out of the high country to meet it: wolves and bears, starving themselves, abandoned every normal wariness of clan settlements and came in close, in numbers no living elder had seen before or would see again. Three children were lost to a single winter's raids before the clan's hunters finally broke the pattern. No single cause of the famine was ever agreed on. It's this decade, not any single sermon, that the Long Grief's earliest teachers pointed to when they began telling people the ground remembers what's done to it — and it's this decade the clan of Meluen's few descendants still mean when they say a hungry world doesn't ask who it eats.

**BYT 5600 — The First Tethering** *[stub — Creature]*
The earliest surviving account of an Antem-Stillwing bond — not the *first* bond, almost certainly, but the first anyone thought worth writing down. A herder named Naeril is said to have refused to eat for three days rather than leave an injured mount to die alone, driving off a circling hawk that had already begun testing the creature's weakness. Whether this is history or the origin myth history borrowed from is genuinely unrecoverable.

**BYT 5100 — The Salt Line War** *[MAJOR — War/Creature]*
Fought over a stretch of coastal flats good for nothing but the salt beneath them, the Salt Line War is the first Antem conflict with a name, a start date, and an end date every surviving account agrees on — which is exactly why scholars treat it with suspicion; everything about it is a little too clean. Two clans, Iowen's and Ranu's, fought a dozen seasons over flats that could have fed neither side's ambitions for more than a generation, made worse by wild boar herds displaced by the fighting turning to raiding both sides' stores indiscriminately, hungry and increasingly bold as the campaign dragged on. It ended not in victory but in exhaustion — both clans sued for the same terms within a season of each other, apparently without knowing the other had done the same. The salt flats sit unclaimed and unworked to this day, out of what modern Antem call, half-joking, "Iowen-Ranu manners" — a reflex to abandon anything two parties have bled over equally.

**BYT 4700 — The Sleeping Door** *[MAJOR — Strange Find/Creature/Genesis Subtext]*
A clan called the Ammerin found a structure on their hillside too regular to be stone alone — seamless, unweathered, warm at its center in a way no rock should be — and rather than build over it or break it open, spent three generations guarding it as a threshold no one was meant to cross. The fourth generation's eldest son crossed it anyway, alone, on a dare he never should have taken seriously. He did not come back. Neither did the two cousins who went in after him when he didn't return by morning. What the searchers found at dawn was the door standing open, the chamber beyond entirely empty, and the ground for a stone's throw in every direction gone the color of an old bruise — a color that didn't fade back to green for the better part of a year. Spiders moved into the bruised ground within the season in numbers no forester had catalogued there before, thick-bodied and pale, and hunters who worked the edge of that patch for the next generation reported them uncommonly aggressive toward anything that lingered. The Ammerin abandoned the hillside within the year. No one has built in sight of that ground since.

**BYT 4180 — The Eye Opens** *[MAJOR — Supernatural/Creature]*
The event the Eye Unclosed faith would eventually be built around: a night, remembered across multiple unconnected clan traditions with startling consistency, when something in the deep sky opened — described variously as a blister of light, a wound, an unblinking eye — and did not close again for the better part of a fortnight. Crops reportedly grew wrong-shaped that season. Bats abandoned their roosts entirely for the duration, screaming through open sky at every hour rather than only dusk, and did not return to normal behavior until the light finally closed. What makes this event unusual isn't the phenomenon itself, which no two tellings agree on in detail, but the fact that it is remembered *at all* with this much consistency across clans who, by every other account, had no contact with one another at the time. Modern Chronicle-Keepers consider this one of the strongest pieces of evidence that something in the old sky genuinely happened on a fixed, unromantic schedule — and privately note the interval, whatever it was, has never been confirmed to repeat, though "every five thousand years, give or take" has become a favorite unprovable claim of the faith's modern remnant, the idiom-keepers of "the Eye doesn't close."

**BYT 3950 — The Liar's Field** *[stub — Anecdote]*
A minor folk-tale, almost certainly retrofitted from a later era: a man who swore a false oath under open sky is said to have gone blind in one eye by morning, in a village that had never heard of the Eye Unclosed doctrine at the time it's supposedly set. What makes the story worth keeping isn't its truth, which nobody defends anymore, but its afterlife — "swearing under open sky" survives today as a real, still-practiced oath-form among several Antem clans, treated as more binding than any oath sworn indoors.

**BYT 3620 — The Binding of the Old Council** *[MAJOR — Power-Shift]*
For roughly a century, several dozen scattered Antem clans loosely federated under a shared council seat — not a kingdom, nothing so formal, but a genuine confederation, the largest political unit the Antem would achieve before the Rivening tore the idea apart for good. It is remembered now mostly for how it ended: not in war, not in betrayal, but in simple erosion — clan by clan quietly stopped sending representatives, and nobody can name the year the council seat finally sat empty. There was no collapse to point to, no single villain, no final battle. It just stopped, the way a fire goes out when nobody's tending it. Some Chronicle-Keepers cite the Old Council as proof the Antem were never suited to centralized rule; others argue it's proof they simply never needed it enough to bother maintaining it.

**BYT 3300 — The Turning Flight** *[stub — Creature]*
For one migration season, every Stillwing bonded to the eastern clans flew toward an oncoming storm instead of away from it — the opposite of every pattern any rider had ever trusted. The Ilsev-kin heeded it and moved their whole camp inland two days before the storm broke; the neighboring Dossa camp, who called it a fluke of bad training, did not, and lost their low grounds and seven people to flooding that the Ilsev-kin watched from higher ground, untouched. Neither behavior has repeated since — but "trust the wing before the water" remains standing Antem storm-lore, credited to a single season no living rider has seen for themselves.

**BYT 2980 — The House of Embers Wakes the Sun** *[MAJOR — Self-Destruction/Creature/Genesis Subtext]*
A minor Kin lineage — the Embers, by the closest translation of a name otherwise lost — held, for two generations, a sealed vessel none of them fully understood, inherited from an ancestor who'd found it in ruin-ground and mistaken its warmth for a blessing. They kept it at the center of their hold, close enough that their children were raised sleeping near its glow. In the third generation, without warning any surviving account can explain, it stopped merely glowing. What remained of the Embers' hold was a crater and a scorch radius wide enough that nothing has grown properly there since, more than fifty-eight centuries later. Salamanders now nest in the crater's warm, ash-fine soil in numbers found nowhere else in the region, apparently thriving on residual heat that outlasted the blast by millennia — the earliest record anywhere in Faerlan's history of a creature colony directly tied to a Genesis-tech scar rather than natural terrain. Antem tellings call the crater a punishment for hoarding a god's fire. What it almost certainly was, unknowable to anyone who told the story, was a power core reaching the end of a containment it was never meant to hold that long in unshielded hands.

**BYT 2750 — The Circle at Vann's Rest** *[MAJOR — Supernatural/Creature]*
A hunting party of five left a Farhand waystation called Vann's Rest and never returned. Search parties found their belongings four days later, arranged — not scattered, arranged — in a perfect ring around a patch of bare, blackened ground, with no bodies, no blood, and no tracks leading away. What the search party did find, circling the blackened patch at a wary distance and refusing to approach closer, was a scattering of pale, drifting shapes the lead tracker's own account calls "wraiths, though none of us had ever seen a wraith be patient before." They didn't attack. They didn't have to; nobody present was willing to test what would happen if they did. The site remains one of the oldest attested "clean vanishings" in Antem record — and the earliest documented sighting of wraiths behaving less like ambient haunting and more like something standing guard.

**BYT 2540 — Counting Down to Nothing** *[MAJOR — Revolution]*
For less than a decade, in one region only, the earliest form of the Twenty-Three Unmakings briefly stopped being a fringe doomsday sect and actually seized real local authority — a handful of villages, terrified into compliance by a charismatic counter who insisted he could name the exact day the world would unmake itself, and had already correctly predicted, by his own account, two minor floods and a bad harvest. He was wrong about the day. He kept revising it. By the fourth revision, the villages that had once handed him their grain stores and their obedience simply stopped showing up to hear the new date, and he is recorded, in the single surviving unflattering account, as having died alone, still counting, certain the next date would be the real one.

**BYT 2310 — The Singing Wall** *[stub — Strange Find/Creature/Genesis Subtext]*
A stretch of buried stone humming at a single, unchanging pitch, cold to the touch in every season. Three separate attempts to break through it are documented; each ended the same way — tools ruined, and within days every mason on the crew reporting the same headache that wouldn't lift for a full turn of the moon, along with small, luminous vent sprites emerging from the cracked stone each time work began, agitated and unwilling to be driven off until the digging stopped. After the third attempt, the local clan-elders forbade further digging by decree — an Antem community legislating against curiosity outright, rather than simply losing interest in it the way every earlier such site had been left alone. The wall was built over deliberately, not forgotten, a distinction the record is careful to preserve even if the exact location wasn't.

**BYT 2105 — Waiting in the Field** *[stub — Revolution/Anecdote]*
The full collapse of the Unmakings cult, remembered less as a tragedy than as one of Antem oral history's favorite jokes at its own expense: the final Unmaking-date, publicly and confidently set decades earlier, arrived on an unremarkable morning, and nothing happened. No flood, no fire, no unmaking. The remaining faithful reportedly spent the morning waiting in a field, in their best clothes, and simply — eventually — went home. The sect never formally dissolved. It just stopped being taken seriously by anyone, including, within a generation, most of its own descendants. "Waiting in the field" survives today as an idiom for pointless certainty.

## 3. THE AGE OF CLANS — BYT 2100 to BYT 1000
*Antem-only, still no Faerl contact. 14 events — 8 MAJOR, 6 stub. (3 of these — the Scattering Wars trio at the end — were relocated here from a later section; see the note above Hawks Over Water.)*

**BYT 2040 — The Bone Fields of Iowen-Ranu** *[MAJOR — Creature]*
Within two generations of the Salt Line War's exhausted peace, the abandoned battlefield itself became a problem neither clan had anticipated: skeletons and wights rose from the unburied dead in numbers no one had troubled to prevent, since neither Iowen's nor Ranu's descendants wanted to claim the ground long enough to properly bury the other side's fallen. The two clans finally cooperated on something for the first time since the war itself — a joint, one-time burial expedition, grudging and silent, that cleared the worst of it. A handful of wights are still occasionally reported drifting the old flats. Nobody's organized a second expedition since; the first one cost both sides more goodwill than either wanted to spend twice.

**BYT 1870 — The Treanted Wood of Greenreach** *[stub — Creature]*
A stand of old forest that had stood undisturbed for as long as any clan's memory reached began, over a single generation, to move — slow, deliberate, and only ever toward anyone attempting to clear it. Treants, unnamed in any Antem tongue at the time, ended three separate logging attempts by three separate clans before the wood was simply left alone by unanimous, unspoken local agreement. It remains uncut today.

**BYT 1740 — The Long Quarrel** *[MAJOR — War/Creature]*
Not one war but four, fought across four generations between descendants of clans that could no longer articulate, by the final round, exactly what the original grievance had been. Each generation's leaders inherited the fight along with the land, adding new grievances atop old ones nobody could untangle anymore — and each war left behind more unburied dead than the last, until ghouls became a standing hazard on both sides' contested ground, scavenging battlefields faster than either clan's dead could be properly honored. It ended, finally, not in resolution but in exhaustion so total that the last two clan-heads are recorded as having simply refused to fight a fifth time, regardless of what their advisors wanted — an Antem leader unilaterally ending an inherited war rather than passing it to the next generation, rather than the other way around.

**BYT 1600 — The Watcher's Bargain** *[stub — Creature]*
A clan besieged by a persistent poison dart sentinel nest near their water source struck an unlikely truce with it rather than continue losing hunters — leaving a portion of every kill at the nest's edge in exchange for being left alone at the well. The arrangement held for two generations before the clan itself dissolved in the Scattering era, and no one since has dared test whether the sentinels still honor a bargain nobody living made.

**BYT 1550 — The Watchers of the High Stone** *[MAJOR — Creature]*
The first recorded death attributed to the birds that would, millennia later, be named Cragwings: a young hunter named Ossel, testing his nerve against clan elders' warnings, climbed toward a nesting shelf on a dare and was found at the cliff's base that evening, the birds still circling above without ever having descended. Whether they killed him or simply startled him into falling was disputed even at the time — but the clan's response wasn't. Disturbing that nesting ground was made a banishable offense within the season, a taboo Antarkeia-era Antem would still be honoring in nearly identical terms three thousand years later.

**BYT 1480 — The Serpent Census** *[stub — Creature]*
A minor but oddly precise piece of surviving record: a clan scribe's tally of rattlesnake sightings across a single decade, rising sharply enough that the clan relocated its main camp half a day's walk from its previous site. No cause is given. The tally itself is the only reason the relocation is remembered at all — a bureaucratic accident of record-keeping outliving the actual event it was tracking.

**BYT 1390 — Forty Days Unfed** *[MAJOR — Supernatural]*
The Long Fast Prophet's actual ordeal, as best as it can be reconstructed — and it cannot be reconstructed cleanly. At least three incompatible versions survive: one in which he wandered the desert alone by choice, seeking answers; one in which he was cast out by his own clan and simply refused to die on schedule; one, favored by later hostile tellings, in which there was no fast at all, only a man who vanished for a season and returned with a story convenient enough to make him powerful. What all three agree on is that he came back changed, teaching that hunting demons was covenant rather than survival — and that whatever actually happened to him in that gap, nobody left alive can say with confidence which version, if any, is true.

**BYT 1310 — The Mudflat Dead** *[stub — Creature]*
A river-adjacent clan lost eleven people over two seasons to what they initially blamed on drowning — until a survivor of the twelfth incident described being pulled under by something that gripped rather than merely swept. Mud lurkers, undocumented before this, are cited in every account since. The clan relocated its crossing point and never used the old ford again.

**BYT 1240 — The Second Prophet** *[stub — Supernatural]*
A lesser-known figure attempted to repeat the Long Fast Prophet's ordeal deliberately, walking into the same stretch of desert a generation later, hoping for the same authority. He never walked back out. A trader who found his abandoned camp two months on — bedroll, waterskins, a cookfire long cold, nothing touched or looted — spread the story that the man had simply given up and slunk home in shame. That version is the one that survives in most tellings, and it's very likely a mercy rather than a fact: nothing about an untouched camp explains where a living man actually went.

**BYT 1150 — The Overturning at Sallowmere** *[MAJOR — Revolution]*
A rare documented instance of a Precursor-descended council — one of the last true holdouts of the old confederated governance style the Old Council had briefly modeled millennia earlier — being deposed not by outside conquest but by its own upstart clan leaders, tired of ceremonial authority with no teeth. The overthrow was reportedly bloodless; the deposed council simply found itself with no one left willing to enforce its rulings, and dissolved within a season. What replaced it was smaller, more local, and more heavily armed — a shift toward exactly the fragmented clan structure that would leave the Antem so vulnerable when both the Long Cold's refugees and, later, the Hollowing arrived. Historians treat Sallowmere as the last gasp of centralized Antem governance before the modern era's near-total absence of it.

**BYT 1050 — Hawks Over Water** *[stub — Creature]*
In the final years before the first Faerling refugee boats reached Faerlan's shores, hunters across several unconnected coastal clans reported hawks refusing to fly out over open water at all — circling back the moment they crossed the shoreline, no matter how they were trained or pressed. No clan connected this to anything at the time. It's remembered now, with the benefit of hindsight nobody had, as the closest thing to a warning the land ever gave before everything changed.

**The Scattering Wars, relocated here from a later draft's misplacement — three entries, all Antem-only, no Faerl involved.** Earlier material dated these to the immediate lead-up to Antarkeia, which no longer works now that the march happens thousands of years later. They belong here instead — the last true Antem-internal conflict before Faerl arrival, matching the "Scattering era" already referenced above.

**The Kindling Years** *[MAJOR — War/Creature]*
This is where the era genuinely earns the name Scattering Wars — not one conflict but several, breaking out among unconnected Antem clans within the same handful of years, none over the same grievance, none aware the others were happening. What made this stretch different from every earlier round of clan violence was what came after the fighting stopped: zombies, risen from battlefields too numerous and too scattered for any single clan to properly bury its own dead, began appearing in numbers that outpaced anything the era's chroniclers had a framework for. No clan connected the wars to the rising dead at the time. In hindsight, the pattern is obvious — a world losing the discipline to honor its own losses was, without meaning to, teaching something worse how to use them.

**The Skirmish at Yalume Ford** *[stub — War]*
A minor engagement, forgettable in scale, between two Antem clans contesting a ford — except one of the two combatants was a young commander not yet called by the name history would remember him under. He won, barely, and is said to have kept the losing side's banner as a personal trophy for the rest of his life. Later chroniclers, working backward from the Unbroken Terms, treat Yalume Ford as the first evidence of Iskrel Sixhold's tactics — a man already fighting like someone who intended to stop fighting eventually.

**A Dead Man's Leash** *[MAJOR — Power-Shift]*
Within five years of Iskrel Sixhold's death, the largest of his former holds repudiated the Unbroken Terms outright, executing an arbitrator sent under its authority and declaring the dead commander's peace "a dead man's leash." No other hold intervened. The Terms survived anyway — not because anyone enforced them, but because enough smaller clans, watching what naked repudiation had cost that hold in allies within a year, quietly kept honoring an agreement nobody could any longer compel them to. It is the clearest evidence Chronicle-Keepers have that the Unbroken Terms were never law. They were reputation, wearing law's clothes, and reputation is what actually persisted.

## 3B. THE SPARSE CONTACT CENTURIES — scattered incidents across deep prehistory

Faerl arrival on Faerlan doesn't happen at one clean date — it's a slow trickle of small, disconnected landings spread across a very long stretch of deep time, all of it comfortably before the Hollowing. What follows are the individual incidents that survive from that span, presented roughly in the order they're believed to fall (very roughly seven thousand down to just over five thousand years before Year Zero), without pretending to a precision the record doesn't support.

Read together, these are **not** a sustained relationship building toward coexistence — an earlier draft of this document read them that way, and that reading is retired. Each incident here is closer to an isolated flicker: one family, one camp, one clan, meeting one other group of strangers, once, sometimes twice, and then going generations without any contact worth recording again. The Faerl in every one of these incidents are still what the arc calls for at this stage — small, wary, under-equipped, and functionally invisible to the wider Antem world, which barely registers them as more than a curiosity or a nuisance.

**The First Watching.** Antem hunters working the northern coast reported the newcomers for months before any contact was made — small, wary camps that broke and moved often, never staying anywhere long enough to be worth approaching. The earliest Antem word for them, in the trade-pidgin that would later develop, translates roughly to "the driftwood people" — not unkind, just accurate. Nobody at the time imagined the driftwood people would still be here millennia later, let alone that their descendants would eventually rename the whole of Vaelume after themselves.

**The Trade at Elder's Reach.** Antem and Faerl exchanging goods, rather than simply avoiding one another, on record for the first time — a small clan, curious rather than hostile, traded dried fish and woven cloth for a handful of Faerl-forged iron tools the clan had never seen the like of before. The exchange repeated itself a handful more times over the following years, never formalized, never guaranteed, and stopped entirely within a generation as both sides drifted back out of each other's range. Chronicle-Keepers still cite Elder's Reach as proof that hostility wasn't the only possible outcome of contact this early — even if, in the end, it was never given room to become anything more than this one brief exchange.

**The Grain Winter.** A multi-year cold snap devastated both Antem grazing land and a scattering of fragile early Faerl camps simultaneously — the first crisis to hit both peoples at once rather than one and not the other. It produced what's remembered now as this whole span's single friendliest moment: a couple of documented instances of an Antem clan sharing stores with a nearby Faerl camp that had nothing to trade in return, and one joint effort to clear a hound den threatening both. It was brief, isolated, and almost entirely forgotten by both descendant cultures within a few centuries, buried under everything that came after.

**The Toll of the Green Road.** Faerl loggers pushing a small timber claim into forest fringe still nominally Antem ground found the work slower than expected — goblin raiding parties harassed the work for the better part of a decade before the loggers began leaving a portion of felled timber at the forest edge as an unofficial toll. It worked. The practice is the quiet, long-forgotten ancestor of a modern custom or two, its actual origin lost along with everyone who knew it.

**The Return at Spearpoint.** A small Faerl party, unfamiliar with Antem mourning custom and desperate for anything valuable to trade through a lean season, broke into a sealed Antem ancestor-ground and stripped it of grave goods before any clan discovered the intrusion. The offended clan wanted nothing but the items returned, which they were, in full, within the month. It's a rare instance of a group of Faerl policing their own specifically to avoid provoking a neighbor they had every reason to fear.

**The Elmsworth Ford Incident.** An armed skirmish between Antem and Faerl — the earliest one on any surviving record, small and forgettable by the standards of what would eventually come, a handful dead on each side, fought over a river crossing neither side had previously bothered to contest. Both sides, by surviving account, seemed genuinely startled it had happened at all, and the crossing was left mutually unused for generations afterward.

**The Ridgeway Party's Toll.** A small Faerl party, ambitious and poorly prepared, attempted to cross a mountain pass into highland territory they didn't know. They didn't account for what Antem hunters in the region already avoided by long-standing, unspoken rule: a resident storm caller whose presence reliably preceded violent, localized weather no ordinary forecasting could predict. Fewer than half made it back down.

**The Boiling of Lake Ilmere.** Without warning, over a single night, a lake supporting a handful of scattered Faerl camps and an Antem fishing clan rose to a temperature that killed nearly everything living in it. Both peoples abandoned the lakeshore within the year and never fully resettled it — one more incident that ends in both sides simply walking away from each other rather than toward anything.

## 4. THE AGE OF MAN — the Faerl rise across the gap

*This section merges and finally redates what were previously two separate placeholder sections (the old "Long Encroachment" and the Faerl-only remainder of "Age of Sorrows"). All 18 entries below are purely Faerl-internal — no Antem involvement — and are now spread across the roughly 5,000-year span between Antem extinction and the Antarkeia breach, per the locked arc: primitive survivors → houses and feuds → a coalition capable of marching on Antarkeia. Dates are deliberately fuzzy and approximate, consistent with this whole document's conventions — treat the phase an entry sits in as far more load-bearing than its specific year. One placement was checked against an existing cross-reference: Colehurst's War Debt (now in the Hollowing/Rivening document, dated to the Rivening) says the house had "already perfected" its forge-debt pattern "generations earlier" — so The First Forge-Debt below is placed early enough to satisfy that.*

### Early Rise (roughly 5,150 to 4,000 years before Year Zero)

**The First Forge-Debt.** A minor Colehurst ancestor, holding little but the coal his own house-founder had kept unexplained for generations, began lending fire and forge-labor to neighboring smallholds in exchange for a cut of every harvest those forges helped produce — not conquest, just leverage, offered freely and repaid, at first, gladly. Within thirty years, every forge within two days' ride owed Colehurst something. It's the earliest surviving example of the pattern that would eventually define the house entirely: reach offered as generosity, and collected later as obligation.

**Landfall on a Borrowed Shore.** The losing faction of Faerling's worst Long Cold split didn't stay to be finished off — they took what boats they could crew and went south along the coast, generation replacing generation aboard vessels never meant for a voyage this long. What finally made landfall on Vaelume's shore, generations after the first boat launched, was not an invading force. It was survivors of survivors, few in number, carrying nothing but each other and a name — Faerl — for a homeland none of them had ever personally seen. *(Note: this may overlap with the arrival already described in Section 0's Faerl Origin Story — worth reconciling the two accounts in a future pass rather than assuming they're describing separate events.)*

**The Sand That Swallowed the Second Camp.** A group of Faerl, pushing inland in search of ground the coast hadn't given them, made camp on what looked like firm desert flats and lost fourteen people in a single night to a den of sand serpents disturbed by their digging. The handful who made it back carried a lesson the rest of the Faerl took seriously for generations: the interior wasn't empty ground waiting to be claimed.

**The Buried Roof.** Early Faerl settlers built a central longhouse directly atop what they took for bedrock, without digging further. In the third generation, the ground gave way in a single night, swallowing four sleepers into a pit a sinkhole lurker had apparently been living beneath the whole time. The settlement relocated and never spoke of why.

**The Vault of House Oswick.** An early, minor Faerl proto-house — never one of the four that would eventually matter, extinct within a single generation — built its entire claim to authority around a sealed chamber its founder discovered and never opened, only displayed. His son, less patient, opened it. What happened next left no bodies to bury and no chamber left standing. The name Oswick survives solely as a regional curse-word for greed that outreaches its own understanding.

**Ossin Walks the Debt.** A Farhand guide led a merchant party safely through dangerous desert ground and was refused his fee at the far end. He's said to have walked back into the desert alone and cursed the route rather than the men who'd wronged him. The route acquired a documented, unusually high rate of traveler deaths for two centuries afterward.

**The Quiet Split.** A handful of dissenting voices broke from a newly founded, unnamed mother church within a decade of its founding, rejecting its teaching that demons existed to test the faithful and preaching instead that they were simply hungry and mindless. The breakaway vanishes from record within two generations. Chronicle-Keepers today consider it the earliest attested ancestor of their own unromantic tradition.

### The Age of Man — Wars and Houses (roughly 4,000 to 500 years before Year Zero)

*This is the stretch the locked arc calls for explicitly — Game-of-Thrones-flavored, houses rising and feuding, magic present but increasingly rare and legendary rather than everyday. Most of what's below is still standalone flavor rather than a connected political narrative; that connective tissue (which houses fought whom, in what order) is still unwritten and is a natural next piece of work, not attempted here.*

**Highbrand's Warning.** A small band of Faerl prospectors, pushing west toward the smoldering edge of the continent near the Great Vantry Rift's mainland shadow, found ground warm enough to work metal without a forge of their own — and found, too late, that the warmth wasn't geological. Ember spirits killed most of the band within the season. The ground itself, generations later, is exactly the ground House Colehurst would eventually build its fire-forges beside.

**A Drifting Coastline.** Faerl explorers reported an island that appeared on two separate expeditions' charts in slightly different positions each time — an island drifter, unrecognized as a living thing at the time. The coastline remains under-mapped to this day, more from lingering unease than real difficulty.

**The Buried Song.** Faerl herders reported, for two seasons, a faint rhythmic sound from deep within a cave system — "a machine's lullaby," by one account, though the teller had no framework for what a machine was. The cave was sealed by a rockslide the following winter and never reopened.

**Aldermoot's Circling Bird.** A single Cragwing circled a Faerl settlement for the better part of a day without touching down, then flew off and was never seen again. Within the decade, that same settlement produced the earliest teachers of the Ash Vigil, who pointed backward at the bird as the first sign the land had taken notice of them.

**The Tally of the Nine Ledgers.** Nine Faerl trading families, scattered and unconnected, are recorded independently adopting the same counting method within a few years of each other — a coincidence with no agreed explanation. It became the standard reckoning still taught to merchant apprentices today.

**The Emptying of Hallow's Bend.** A border village of some forty Faerl was found entirely without its people — homes standing, meals left mid-preparation, every dyed cloth and painted door gone the same washed-grey nothing-color. Nothing was ever recovered. "Gone grey as Hallow's Bend" still describes anything drained past the point of ordinary loss.

**The Warding of the Hollow Root.** A Thorne-adjacent clan, unable to clear a hedge hound den, began leaving meat at the base of the tree each equinox instead. It worked well enough that the practice outlived both the clan and the den, absorbed wholesale into modern Thorne vassal custom as simple reverence, the original fear long forgotten.

**The Comb of Bone.** A grave-good recovered from an unmarked burial — a comb carved from something that wasn't bone, in a script no scholar since has been able to place. The one researcher who tried seriously, Colehurst-sponsored, stopped publishing abruptly, leaving notes describing a recurring headache and a conviction the script "wasn't meant to be read by anyone who'd still want to read it after." The comb sits uncatalogued in a private Colehurst collection today.

### Consolidation (roughly 500 to 0 years before Year Zero)

*The final stretch — houses that have survived this long start behaving like the paramount powers the Chronicle of Antarkeia and the Hollowing/Rivening document already describe converging on the vault.*

**Vandrick's Salt.** A minor trade dispute over salt-tax between two small Faerl holdings, remembered now only because the ruling that settled it — that salt could never be taxed above what a single household could carry by hand — quietly became precedent, and still shapes how salt is priced across every Faerl market today.

**The Silence of the Second Bell.** A Faerl chapel bell, rung twice daily without fail for over a century, went silent for a single dusk with no mechanical fault anyone could find, and rang again the next dawn as if nothing had happened. An echo stalker is the leading modern explanation — the earliest suspected case of the species interfering with sound rather than mimicking it.

**The Empty Chair at Sallow End.** A minor Thorne vassal hall holds a formal seat at its high table, set and served at every feast, for a guest who never arrived and was never named. The custom persists to this day. No one currently serving there can say who it was originally set for.

## 5. THE AGE UNWRITTEN — YT 0 to YT 275 (working title — "The Unbound Age" also under consideration, neither locked)
*The medieval structure that carried Faerl through their rise does not survive contact with the demons Antarkeia's breach released — not a clean collapse into chaos, but a genuine regression, with no ruling houses left to hold things together and the fate of Faerlish society still swinging either direction. The 30 entries below (unchanged from the prior draft, not yet reread individually against this reframing) already lean toward exactly this tone — monster-hunting institutions forming, communities adapting and continuing rather than simply dying, mysteries accumulating — more than they lean toward outright doom. Retitled to match; a full entry-by-entry tone pass has not been done, and probably isn't needed given how well most of it already fits.*

**YT 4 — The Boy Who Lived to Tell It** *[MAJOR — Demon]*
The border hamlet of Thistleford was found in ruin four days after a traveling tinker last passed through it whole — timber charred in patterns that matched no fire anyone could reconstruct, a dozen dead where two dozen had lived, and one survivor: a boy named Renn Coldwell, alive because he'd been hiding from a scolding when it happened. What he described — a shape the wrong color for anything that should burn, moving through the wreckage without ever quite touching it — was, for the first time in the world's memory, believed. Not because his account was clean; it wasn't, and never fully cohered. It was believed because everyone alive still remembered Antarkeia, four years gone, and Cael Thorncrest's stopped count, and understood, collectively, that the age of nobody surviving to describe it had just ended. Renn Coldwell spent the rest of his life being asked to tell the story and never once told it the same way twice. The Bound cite him as their founding witness anyway.

**YT 9 — The First Lodge at Ferncrest** *[stub — Institution]*
A handful of Antarkeia veterans, unwilling to simply return to farming after what they'd survived, pooled what little coin they had and built a single shared hall dedicated to nothing but training and readiness — no doctrine yet, no formal oath, just men and women who'd rather prepare together than wait alone. It predates the Reckoning Faith's formal rise by over a decade and is, per most Bound histories, the actual root the faith grew doctrine onto after the fact.

**YT 14 — Duskmoor Wood** *[MAJOR — Demon/Ecological]*
A stretch of forest bordering a minor Thorne vassal died over the course of twelve days — not burned, not blighted in any pattern a forester recognized, just steadily greyer from a single point outward until nothing green remained in a mile's radius. Two woodcutters who went in partway through to investigate didn't come back. What did come back, near the end, was the color itself — every dyed cloth and painted door in the nearest village losing its hue overnight, in a pattern the older among them recognized immediately from stories out of the Hollowing era, though none of them had a word for what they were recognizing. The Bound's earliest formal chapter records list Duskmoor Wood as their second confirmed demon incident, and the first one anyone thought to write down while it was still happening rather than after.

**YT 24 — The Well Nobody Kept** *[MAJOR — Demon/War]*
A minor border dispute between two small holdings — the kind of thing that happened constantly and settled itself within a season — escalated into an actual skirmish over a disputed well, some fifty fighters on each side. Neither side is recorded as having won. A demon reportedly moved through the field somewhere in the fighting, and by the time anyone thought to stop swinging long enough to look, both sides had lost roughly equal numbers to something that wasn't the other side's blades. Survivors from both holdings gave up the well entirely rather than ever contest it again. It's the earliest case on record of a demon effectively ending a Faerl-on-Faerl conflict by making the original stakes look small — a pattern the Bound would come to call, grimly, "getting reminded."

**YT 33 — The Faces at Millstead** *[MAJOR — Creature/Demon-Adjacent]*
A trade town of some two hundred people simply stopped answering correspondence, and the merchant caravan sent to investigate three weeks later found the gates standing open and nobody left inside who could still be called anybody. What had moved in wore the shapes of people who'd once lived there — a scattering of wights and at least one confirmed Lich, wearing, by the account of the one caravan guard who survived close enough to look, "a face I'd traded with not two summers past." No coordinated cause was ever established for how an entire town turned this thoroughly, this quietly, without a single outside report of struggle. Millstead was sealed by Thorne decree rather than cleared — burning the town was debated and rejected, on the grounds that no one could say for certain fire would actually finish whatever this was. It stands empty today, technically unclaimed, and no formal expedition has gone back in over a century.

**YT 38 — The Historian Who Wrote It Twice** *[MAJOR — Creature/Genesis Subtext]*
A minor Chronicle-Keeper archive in a river town was found, on a routine inspection, to contain two versions of the same decade's records — identical in every particular except for small, specific details that had been quietly altered: a death moved a year earlier, a name changed, a cause of death softened. No forger was ever caught, no motive established, and the older archivists on staff swore, with visible unease, that neither version was the one they remembered writing. The creature later cataloged as the Chronicle Watcher — first formally described nearly a century afterward, in a completely different archive, doing precisely the same thing — wasn't connected to this incident until long after anyone from the original town was alive to confirm it. Chronicle-Keepers now treat any archive with an unexplained duplicate record as a live incident, not a filing error, and quietly review such archives for exactly this pattern before trusting anything in them.

**YT 44 — The Waking of the Deep Cut** *[stub — Creature]*
A Colehurst mining operation, digging deeper than any local shaft had gone before, broke into a sealed chamber and woke something the miners' own foreman described only as "a wall that stood up." The **Standing Wall** — distinct from the Desert's Stone Colossus, though the two are related closely enough in shape that Chronicle-Keepers have argued for years over whether they share an origin — collapsed the shaft entirely, killing four, and hasn't been provoked since. The mine was abandoned, and Colehurst quietly stopped funding deep excavation in that district for two full generations.

**YT 52 — Featherfall's Echo** *[stub — Creature]*
A different flock of Cragwings, in a different region entirely from the Antarkeia-era Winoa Featherfall incident, was recorded refusing to scream at an approaching hunting party for reasons no one present could explain. Naturalists remain split on whether this is coincidence, a real behavioral pattern nobody's identified the trigger for, or simple confirmation bias reading meaning into silence.

**YT 71 — Too Many Eyes** *[stub — Creature]*
Treasure-hunters who broke into a sealed desert tomb didn't return; what did return, weeks later, wandering half-starved into a nearby outpost, was one survivor who couldn't explain how he'd gotten out or what had happened to the other three, only that "something with too many eyes counted us in and only let one back out." The Guardian Scarab swarm responsible has never been successfully cleared from that tomb since.

**YT 78 — No Tracks at Dawn** *[MAJOR — Demon-Adjacent/Creature]*
A merchant caravan of eight wagons and some thirty travelers vanished crossing a stretch of open desert with no cover, no known bandit activity, and — critically — no tracks leading away from where the caravan's last confirmed campsite was found, fires still smoldering. Sandstorm Flankers and at least one Mirage Hunter are the leading theory, based on hoofprint patterns found nearby that led nowhere useful, but no confirmed body or survivor account has ever settled it. The route is still used today; the story is still told to every caravan crossing it, mostly, by this point, as a way of telling new drivers to keep their wagons close together after dark.

**YT 83 — Root and Ruin** *[stub — Creature]*
A stand of forest near a Thorne timber camp began, over a single growing season, sprouting root systems that moved when unwatched and struck when disturbed — Root Sprites, in numbers no forester had previously documented in one place. Two loggers were killed before the camp abandoned the grove entirely. It's marked on modern Thorne maps simply as "the teeth," with no further label needed for anyone local.

**YT 87 — The Guest Who Wore Her Face** *[MAJOR — Creature/Supernatural]*
A minor noble estate maintained, for reasons its own family had long since forgotten, a chamber with two facing mirrors set an exact, deliberate distance apart — an old custom, harmless as far as anyone knew, until the family's youngest daughter stepped between them on a dare and something stepped back out that answered to her name, wore her face, and knew things about her that only she should have known. The two were indistinguishable for four days before the household's old nurse noticed the copy flinched at a story the real girl had never actually been told. What became of either the girl or the thing that replaced her isn't recorded past that point — the family sealed the chamber and never spoke of it again, and it passed into regional legend as the Room of Two Mirrors, still occasionally rumored to exist somewhere, its actual location lost along with whichever family it belonged to.

**YT 92 — Locked, Not Lost** *[MAJOR — Self-Destruction/Genesis Subtext]*
House Latchpit, whose own motto had always been "locked, not lost," held for two generations a sealed cylinder recovered from ruin-ground during the family's founding — kept exactly as found, out of what the family itself described as prudence rather than fear. The second generation's heir, ambitious and unwilling to inherit only caution, had it forced open by a hired locksmith against his own father's dying wish. What followed left the family seat a crater and killed everyone inside it, the locksmith included, in a flash bright enough to be seen from two neighboring vassal halls. House Latchpit's motto survives its own extinction as regional irony — "locked, not lost" now said, dryly, of anyone who insists on opening something they were explicitly warned not to.

**YT 97 — The Third Day at Aldergate** *[MAJOR — Demon/War]*
Deep in the Smoke Year War's worst fighting, a Colehurst relief column and a rebel vassal force met at Aldergate for what should have been a routine engagement — until, by multiple surviving accounts from both sides, something moved through the middle of the field and neither side had a living soldier left standing within reach of it by the time it was gone. Both commanders, technically enemies, are recorded retreating in the same direction at the same time without a word exchanged. Aldergate is remembered less as a battle than as the moment the Smoke Year War's own combatants briefly stopped fighting each other and started fighting the same fear instead — a truce that lasted exactly as long as it took both sides to get out of sight of one another.

**YT 101 — Aldergate's Inheritance** *[stub — Creature]*
In the seasons after Aldergate, the scorched ground around the old battlefield began producing something new — Magma Creepers, never before documented in that region, apparently drawn to or born from the residual heat of a war that had burned the district for the better part of a decade. Local hunters treat the field itself as cursed ground regardless of what's actually causing it.

**YT 109 — The Orphans' Oath** *[stub — Institution]*
In the Smoke Year War's aftermath, a new hunting lodge was founded specifically for war orphans with nowhere else to go — funded, awkwardly, by a small Colehurst reparations fund nobody involved liked discussing openly. It remains, a century and a half later, one of the more quietly effective Bound chapters in the Fire zone, staffed almost entirely by descendants of people the war itself displaced.

**YT 117 — The Silence Above the Cloud Line** *[MAJOR — Creature]*
An expedition of thirteen mapmakers and guides, sent to chart a mountain pass no Faerl survey had reached before, sent one final signal-fire from high ground and were never heard from again. A second party, sent a year later, found their camp intact, equipment untouched, and a single word scratched into a rock face near the site — illegible, worn already by weather, in handwriting nobody could match to any of the thirteen. Summit Titans are the leading explanation, based on ground-disturbance patterns found nearby, though no body has ever been recovered from that pass in the century since. It remains uncharted by choice rather than difficulty.

**YT 124 — Where the Ice Walks** *[stub — Creature]*
A trade route through a mountain ice cave, reliable for two generations, was abandoned within a single season after several consecutive caravans reported losing pack animals to something that moved through the ice itself rather than over it. Frost Creepers and at least one Cave Lurcher are documented in the area since; the route has never reopened.

**YT 142 — The River's Quiet Refusal** *[stub — Creature]*
A minor Marlow river-trade route, unremarkable for generations, was quietly abandoned after successive boats reported cargo — and, twice, crew — pulled under by something beneath the surface that left no wake and no warning. Undertow Shades are the standing explanation among river-folk, though Marlow's own official record simply lists the route as "discontinued," without further comment.

**YT 151 — The Question Yveth Finally Asked** *[MAJOR — Creature/Genesis Subtext]*
A minor desert scholar named Yveth Marrelle spent the better part of two decades studying a sealed ruin-ground structure guarded by something the local Farhand called, dismissively, "the glass thing" — a fused, faceted construct that had reportedly killed or maimed everyone who'd tried to force entry for as long as anyone could remember. Yveth didn't try to force anything. She spent years simply observing it, cataloging its patterns, convinced it was responding to something rather than simply guarding blindly. She was right — and the Glass Shard Golem let her in, alone, on what her own final journal entry calls "the day it finally recognized the question I was actually asking." She never came back out, and the journal, recovered by a nephew who went looking for her years later, ends mid-sentence. The structure has never let anyone else close since.

**YT 160 — The Forest That Went Quiet at Dusk** *[MAJOR — Demon]*
An entire stretch of old-growth Thorne forest, home to two generations of a single logging community, was reduced within a single night to standing ash — trees still upright, bark still intact, but hollowed and grey the way Duskmoor Wood had been over a century and a half earlier. Unlike Duskmoor, this happened fast enough that several loggers sleeping in a nearby camp didn't wake in time to run. What survivors from the wider settlement describe isn't a fire, but a cold that arrived first — a stillness in the air that every animal in earshot fled from an hour before anything else happened. The dead ground is marked on modern maps and in modern flavor text alike simply as the Ashen Reach, and no forester has cut timber within a day's walk of it since.

**YT 175 — The Warden No One Thanked** *[MAJOR — Creature]*
For longer than any living memory could confirm, a solitary creature — recorded only in fragmentary local testimony as the **Ford Sentinel**, territorial and dangerous but never aggressive toward travelers who kept their distance — had discouraged anyone from lingering at a particular ford in Thorne territory, driving off the curious and the careless alike for generations. A hunting party finally killed it in YT 175, proud of clearing "dangerous ground" for safer crossing. Five years later, Kester Dray's horse would stumble on the buried Antem-era technology that killed him, at that exact ford — technology the Ford Sentinel had, without anyone ever understanding why, spent its whole unrecorded life keeping travelers too far back to disturb.

**YT 188 — The Alpha's Reach** *[stub — Creature]*
A wolf pack near a Thorne vassal grew, over several seasons, unnaturally large and unnaturally coordinated for the region — territorial hunting patterns closer to a standing army than ordinary pack behavior. The Alpha Wolf responsible was eventually hunted down by a joint effort of several neighboring holdings, none of which had previously agreed on anything else in living memory.

**YT 205 — The Last Night of Reed Hollow** *[MAJOR — Demon]*
A modest farming village lost its entire population — some ninety people — over the course of a single night, with survivors from a neighboring settlement describing distant screaming that stopped all at once, followed by silence and a smell like scorched copper that lingered for days. Bound investigators who reached Reed Hollow within the week catalogued the scene in detail no earlier incident affords historians — structures, positioning, timing, all on record. The dispute isn't over what happened. It's over what it means that this incident's *signature* doesn't match any prior one closely enough to file as the same threat. Reed Hollow is the incident most modern Bound doctrine points to when arguing demons aren't one consistent threat but many different ones wearing the same fear — a claim the Reckoning Faith's mainstream still resists.

**YT 210 — Culling Season** *[stub — Creature]*
A minor river city suffered an outbreak blamed, initially, on bad water — until a physician noticed the sick had one thing in common: recent contact with an unusually aggressive strain of vermin later cataloged as Scavenger Rats, carrying something closer to venom than ordinary filth. The city's rat-culling ordinances, still enforced today, trace directly back to this outbreak.

**YT 218 — The Hedge Nobody Leaves** *[MAJOR — Creature]*
A hedge maze grown by a wealthy Marlow-adjacent family as a garden novelty developed, over several decades of neglect, something considerably worse than overgrowth — travelers who wandered in reported paths that didn't match on the way back out, and several documented disappearances over a twenty-year span before the family finally condemned the grounds. The Bound's formal report is unusually precise for a case file — names, dates, entry points, all confirmed against household records. What it can't settle isn't whether people went in and failed to come out; that part is documented past dispute. What it can't settle is why the maze waited two generations of neglect before it started keeping anyone at all. Loop Shades and at least one confirmed Instinct Eater are cited as responsible; the hedges have never been cleared, and burning them was attempted twice and abandoned both times when the crews doing the burning couldn't agree, afterward, on how long they'd actually been inside.

**YT 233 — A Debt Paid in Skin** *[stub — Creature]*
A minor medical scandal broke when a healer was found using Restorative Leeches — genuinely effective at drawing out infection, but requiring a live host to feed between treatments — on unwitting patients rather than paying for proper feed-stock. The practice wasn't banned outright, since the leeches' medical value was real, but licensing law since requires disclosed, consenting hosts only.

**YT 245 — The City Preserved** *[MAJOR — Genesis Subtext/Creature]*
A ruin near the Fire zone's edge, half-swallowed by what locals simply call the amber ground — a resin-like substance that preserves whatever it touches with unsettling, unnatural completeness, tools and bones alike looking freshly used centuries later — has resisted every serious excavation attempt since its discovery. Amber Sentinels patrol its edges with a consistency no naturalist has explained, and at least one expedition lost a member to an Overflow Imp that emerged from beneath the resin itself, apparently disturbed by the digging. What the amber is actually preserving, and why, remains the district's most persistent unanswered question — the ruin is locally known as Amber City, though nothing about it has ever been confirmed to have been a city at all.

**YT 258 — The Effigy's Vote** *[MAJOR — Creature/Political]*
A minor noble house's ancestral hall maintained an effigy of its long-dead founder at the head of the great table — tradition, nothing more, until a succession dispute between two feuding heirs saw the effigy reportedly move on its own, siding, by every witness's account, unmistakably with the elder claimant. Court record confirms the succession dispute, the timing, and the younger heir's withdrawal within the month — none of that is in question. What the record can't confirm, and every serving steward at that hall since has declined to speculate on for the recorder, is whether the effigy actually moved, or whether an heir already losing simply needed somewhere else to place the blame for folding. The effigy — now cataloged among the **Seated Ancestors**, a creature type few outside the Bound's stranger case files take seriously — has not moved since, dead weight and painted wood at the head of a table nobody at that hall discusses without lowering their voice first.

**YT 266 — The Basin Keeps Its Own** *[MAJOR — Genesis Subtext/Creature]*
A submerged ruin at the bottom of a still, unnaturally deep basin has drawn scattered attention for generations — divers who go deep enough report structures too regular to be natural, and at least one confirmed sighting of a lone, silent figure on the basin's shore matching descriptions of the Non, watching rather than approaching. A **Chronicle Watcher** guards whatever's actually down there; no diver has yet returned with more than fragments, and at least two have not returned at all. Chronicle-Keepers who've studied the basin privately favor the theory that it's an archive of some kind — and privately note that a lone Non watching over an archive nobody Faerl or Antem can read, guarded by exactly the kind of spirit their own order names itself after, is not, historically, the kind of coincidence this world tends to produce.

## 6. ADDED CONNECTIVE THREADS (world-building added beyond the 90 base events)

- **The Embers' crater (BYT 2980) and the Ashen Reach (YT 160)** now share a documented naturalist's cross-reference: a Chronicle-Keeper survey of both sites notes salamander colonies thriving at the Embers' crater but conspicuously absent from the Ashen Reach despite near-identical ground-scarring — the first hard evidence, usable in future flavor text without ever naming the mechanism, that not all Genesis-tech wounds heal into the same kind of scar.
- **Ossin Walks the Debt's cursed route** carries a disputed folk-claim that it's "a lesser cousin of the White Wind, minted local" — a small, deniable example of a newer legend borrowing prestige from Renna Chases the Mirage's older, bigger one. Neither is confirmed connected; that's the point.
- **The Dossa clan** appears twice, three thousand years apart — once ignoring a storm-warning at BYT 3300, once annihilating itself at Dossa's Field in BYT 34 — with the connection left explicitly unconfirmed in the text itself (a name reused by coincidence, or a bloodline with a very long memory for bad decisions).
- **The Ford Sentinel (YT 175)** now gives Ford at Wrenfield a real causal "why now" instead of pure coincidence — the thing that had been keeping travelers at a safe distance from Kester Dray's death for longer than anyone knew was killed five years before he died there.
- **Amber City** and **the Non watching the Basin** convert two previously flagged-but-undocumented ideas from prior sessions into real, usable events.
