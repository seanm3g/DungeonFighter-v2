# Chronicle of the First Child

Source: Claude artifact `cc5d85d1-b980-47da-bfd0-f9ac4fd8e0f5`, retrieved 2026-10-15.
Machine-extracted from the published artifact; the canonical copy lives in Joey's Claude project.


## The Genesis Era

*-63500 → -27450*

### Genesis Detection
`63,500 BY · Genesis Yr 0 · stub · RF · GT`

Earth’s satellite identifies the First Child as habitable. The natives, only in the nascent phases of their growth — first crops perhaps two thousand years past, first domesticated animals a thousand — call the new light in their sky “It Appeared,” because one day it wasn’t there, and then it was. It is still there, a forgotten relic, the way the pyramids would still stand ten thousand years after anyone remembered who built them.

### The Robots Launch
`c. 63,000 BY · GY 500 · stub · RF`

After roughly five centuries of Earth-side preparation, the robots launch — sterile, AI-driven workhorses loaded with every metric Earth’s engineers believed would broker peace between interlopers and inhabitants.

### Androids and Ark 1 Launch
`c. 53,500 – 52,500 BY · stub · RF`

The androids follow some 9,500 years of Earth-side R&D, grown from something closer to a womb than a production line, their faces designed to be approachable and kind. Ark 1 launches alongside them on confidence rather than data — no signal has yet come back from the robots. It carries five thousand of Earth’s best: farmers, engineers, mathematicians, held in cryo-suspension.

### The Gifted Workhorses
`c. 43,000 BY · GY 20,500 · major · RF · GT`

The robots arrive after a twenty-thousand-year crossing and begin the long “gifted workhorse” phase. They plow fields, build dams, raise structures — advancing the native civilization in the cosmic equivalent of overnight. The Precursors (the people current canon calls the Antem) are mesmerized, then inventive. They mount the machines like steeds, tear decommissioned ones apart, and use what the machines taught them to build better machines. They go to war with each other, as people do.

### A Reassuring Signal
`c. 42,300 BY · stub · RF`

The robots’ confirmation signal reaches Ark 1 at roughly forty percent of its journey. It reads as reassuring. It confirms nothing about what is actually happening on the ground.

### The Androids Arrive
`c. 33,500 BY · GY 30,000 · major · RF · GT`

Expecting to shepherd a primitive species, the androids find a fully developed proto-society — Greek in its curiosity and architecture, built on spare robot parts, already aware of the interlopers on their way and already scrambling their data. The Precursors are treated as equals. There is no single disclosure day. Some androids take sides in Precursor wars; some, equipped for it, breed with them.

### The Childlings
`c. 33,500 BY onward · vignette · NT 3`

Android–Precursor unions produce children neither fully mechanical nor fully born — the childlings — and neither side fully claims them.

### Earth’s Illusion Breaks
`c. 33,000 BY · stub · RF`

The androids’ observation data reaches Earth: the mission has gone nothing like planned. Earth reads a species of this intellect as a threat to its own primacy.

### Too Late to Matter
`c. 32,600 BY · stub · RF`

The same data reaches Ark 1 at roughly eighty percent of its journey. The ship is already irrevocably committed.

### The Schism
`c. 33,000 – 32,000 BY · vignette · NT 1 · RF`

A thousand-year civilizational fracture on Earth over what to do with humanity’s own volatility. Emotion-extraction technology is developed in the same window.

### The Payload and Ark 2 Launch
`c. 32,000 BY · GY 31,500 · major · RF · GT`

The extraction faction wins. Every emotion — fear, anger, grief, wonder, all of it — is withdrawn and launched at the First Child as an unmanned payload at roughly eleven percent of light speed, carrying wreckage and debris with it: partly retaliation, partly so Earth could move on, partly so it could ignore its failure.

Those who refused extraction were given a choice: die, or go on Ark 2. Billions were willing; half were exterminated without the others knowing, half starved waiting. The last two percent fought feuds in the labor camps for millennia before Ark 2 finally left, packed with only the most tenacious survivors — a deportation vessel, bare-bones, aimed at nowhere in particular.

### Ark 1 Lands at Antarkeia
`c. 27,500 BY · GY 36,000 · major · RF · HR`

After a twenty-five-thousand-year crossing, Ark 1 comes down at the site that will one day be Antarkeia — the deliberate, prepared landing. Some of the crew try to preserve the mission’s hopeful intent; others arrive already disillusioned. Like the androids before them, they choose: integrate, fight, pick a side. Not every genius is a survivor. The ones who survive become the ancestors of the Non.

### The Crash
`c. 27,450 BY · GY 36,050 · vignette · NT 2 · RF`

The payload arrives, faster than Ark 2 by twenty thousand years. Extracted emotion floods the population as color — grey despair, red-purple violence, pink lust, green greed, yellow joy. Peoples who had been simpatico for millennia begin to murder one another. The planet rips itself apart. This is the origin of demons.


## The Great Forgetting

*-27450 → -12000*

### The Gift Misread
`After the Crash · major · Decision Log`

With no way to understand the bombardment’s hostile origin, the Antem read it as a gift they don’t fully understand. Over generations they tend what emerges from it the way a gardener tends overgrowth — cutting it back, believing they are containing it. They never fight the demons. That passive-containment posture, not any outside force, is their real mistake: sustained proximity slowly corrupts them from within, the deep cause beneath everything that follows. Outwardly, their power stays intact for millennia.

### The Keeper Who Would Not Open the Door
`Deep antiquity · unnumbered · vignette · VA`

Two Non, each holding half of a broken record, cannot agree whether a whole truth should be reassembled. Some Non tellings hold this is why no two Non archives agree to this day.


## The Deep Dream

*-15500 → -12000*

### The Long Hunger
`Legacy BYT 5820 · major · DH`

Before there was a faith called the Long Grief, there was a decade of green things simply failing — wells running backward from every season anyone trusted, herds calving stillborn two years running. The clan of Meluen, largest on the eastern grass, lost enough young that it never again fielded its old numbers. What made the famine unforgettable wasn’t only the hunger, but what the hunger drove down out of the high country to meet it: wolves and bears, starving themselves, abandoned every normal wariness of clan settlements and came in close, in numbers no living elder had seen before or would see again. Three children were lost to a single winter’s raids before the clan’s hunters finally broke the pattern. No single cause of the famine was ever agreed on. It’s this decade, not any single sermon, that the Long Grief’s earliest teachers pointed to when they began telling people the ground remembers what’s done to it — and it’s this decade the clan of Meluen’s few descendants still mean when they say a hungry world doesn’t ask who it eats.

### The First Tethering
`Legacy BYT 5600 · stub · DH`

The earliest surviving account of an Antem-Stillwing bond — not the first bond, almost certainly, but the first anyone thought worth writing down. A herder named Naeril is said to have refused to eat for three days rather than leave an injured mount to die alone, driving off a circling hawk that had already begun testing the creature’s weakness. Whether this is history or the origin myth history borrowed from is genuinely unrecoverable.

### The Salt Line War
`Legacy BYT 5100 · major · DH`

Fought over a stretch of coastal flats good for nothing but the salt beneath them, the Salt Line War is the first Antem conflict with a name, a start date, and an end date every surviving account agrees on — which is exactly why scholars treat it with suspicion; everything about it is a little too clean. Two clans, Iowen’s and Ranu’s, fought a dozen seasons over flats that could have fed neither side’s ambitions for more than a generation, made worse by wild boar herds displaced by the fighting turning to raiding both sides’ stores indiscriminately, hungry and increasingly bold as the campaign dragged on. It ended not in victory but in exhaustion — both clans sued for the same terms within a season of each other, apparently without knowing the other had done the same. The salt flats sit unclaimed and unworked to this day, out of what modern Antem call, half-joking, “Iowen-Ranu manners” — a reflex to abandon anything two parties have bled over equally.

### The Sleeping Door
`Legacy BYT 4700 · major · DH`

A clan called the Ammerin found a structure on their hillside too regular to be stone alone — seamless, unweathered, warm at its center in a way no rock should be — and rather than build over it or break it open, spent three generations guarding it as a threshold no one was meant to cross. The fourth generation’s eldest son crossed it anyway, alone, on a dare he never should have taken seriously. He did not come back. Neither did the two cousins who went in after him when he didn’t return by morning. What the searchers found at dawn was the door standing open, the chamber beyond entirely empty, and the ground for a stone’s throw in every direction gone the color of an old bruise — a color that didn’t fade back to green for the better part of a year. Spiders moved into the bruised ground within the season in numbers no forester had catalogued there before, thick-bodied and pale, and hunters who worked the edge of that patch for the next generation reported them uncommonly aggressive toward anything that lingered. The Ammerin abandoned the hillside within the year. No one has built in sight of that ground since.

### The Eye Opens
`Legacy BYT 4180 · major · DH · CF`

The event the Eye Unclosed faith would eventually be built around: a night, remembered across multiple unconnected clan traditions with startling consistency, when something in the deep sky opened — described variously as a blister of light, a wound, an unblinking eye — and did not close again for the better part of a fortnight. Crops reportedly grew wrong-shaped that season. Bats abandoned their roosts entirely for the duration, screaming through open sky at every hour rather than only dusk, and did not return to normal behavior until the light finally closed. What makes this event unusual isn’t the phenomenon itself, which no two tellings agree on in detail, but the fact that it is remembered at all with this much consistency across clans who, by every other account, had no contact with one another at the time. Modern Chronicle-Keepers consider this one of the strongest pieces of evidence that something in the old sky genuinely happened on a fixed, unromantic schedule — and privately note the interval, whatever it was, has never been confirmed to repeat, though “every five thousand years, give or take” has become a favorite unprovable claim of the faith’s modern remnant, the idiom-keepers of “the Eye doesn’t close.”

### The Liar’s Field
`Legacy BYT 3950 · stub · DH`

A minor folk-tale, almost certainly retrofitted from a later era: a man who swore a false oath under open sky is said to have gone blind in one eye by morning, in a village that had never heard of the Eye Unclosed doctrine at the time it’s supposedly set. What makes the story worth keeping isn’t its truth, which nobody defends anymore, but its afterlife — “swearing under open sky” survives today as a real, still-practiced oath-form among several Antem clans, treated as more binding than any oath sworn indoors.

### The Binding of the Old Council
`Legacy BYT 3620 · major · DH`

For roughly a century, several dozen scattered Antem clans loosely federated under a shared council seat — not a kingdom, nothing so formal, but a genuine confederation, the largest political unit the Antem would achieve before the Rivening tore the idea apart for good. It is remembered now mostly for how it ended: not in war, not in betrayal, but in simple erosion — clan by clan quietly stopped sending representatives, and nobody can name the year the council seat finally sat empty. There was no collapse to point to, no single villain, no final battle. It just stopped, the way a fire goes out when nobody’s tending it. Some Chronicle-Keepers cite the Old Council as proof the Antem were never suited to centralized rule; others argue it’s proof they simply never needed it enough to bother maintaining it.

### The Turning Flight
`Legacy BYT 3300 · stub · DH`

For one migration season, every Stillwing bonded to the eastern clans flew toward an oncoming storm instead of away from it — the opposite of every pattern any rider had ever trusted. The Ilsev-kin heeded it and moved their whole camp inland two days before the storm broke; the neighboring Dossa camp, who called it a fluke of bad training, did not, and lost their low grounds and seven people to flooding that the Ilsev-kin watched from higher ground, untouched. Neither behavior has repeated since — but “trust the wing before the water” remains standing Antem storm-lore, credited to a single season no living rider has seen for themselves.

### The House of Embers Wakes the Sun
`Legacy BYT 2980 · major · DH`

A minor lineage — the Embers, by the closest translation of a name otherwise lost — held, for two generations, a sealed vessel none of them fully understood, inherited from an ancestor who’d found it in ruin-ground and mistaken its warmth for a blessing. They kept it at the center of their hold, close enough that their children were raised sleeping near its glow. In the third generation, without warning any surviving account can explain, it stopped merely glowing. What remained of the Embers’ hold was a crater and a scorch radius wide enough that nothing has grown properly there since, more than fifty-eight centuries later. Salamanders now nest in the crater’s warm, ash-fine soil in numbers found nowhere else in the region, apparently thriving on residual heat that outlasted the blast by millennia — the earliest record anywhere in Faerlan’s history of a creature colony directly tied to a Genesis-tech scar rather than natural terrain. Antem tellings call the crater a punishment for hoarding a god’s fire. What it almost certainly was, unknowable to anyone who told the story, was a power core reaching the end of a containment it was never meant to hold that long in unshielded hands.

### The Circle at Vann’s Rest
`Legacy BYT 2750 · major · DH`

A hunting party of five left a Farhand waystation called Vann’s Rest and never returned. Search parties found their belongings four days later, arranged — not scattered, arranged — in a perfect ring around a patch of bare, blackened ground, with no bodies, no blood, and no tracks leading away. What the search party did find, circling the blackened patch at a wary distance and refusing to approach closer, was a scattering of pale, drifting shapes the lead tracker’s own account calls “wraiths, though none of us had ever seen a wraith be patient before.” They didn’t attack. They didn’t have to; nobody present was willing to test what would happen if they did. The site remains one of the oldest attested “clean vanishings” in Antem record — and the earliest documented sighting of wraiths behaving less like ambient haunting and more like something standing guard.

### Counting Down to Nothing
`Legacy BYT 2540 · major · DH · CF`

For less than a decade, in one region only, the earliest form of the Twenty-Three Unmakings briefly stopped being a fringe doomsday sect and actually seized real local authority — a handful of villages, terrified into compliance by a charismatic counter who insisted he could name the exact day the world would unmake itself, and had already correctly predicted, by his own account, two minor floods and a bad harvest. He was wrong about the day. He kept revising it. By the fourth revision, the villages that had once handed him their grain stores and their obedience simply stopped showing up to hear the new date, and he is recorded, in the single surviving unflattering account, as having died alone, still counting, certain the next date would be the real one.

### The Singing Wall
`Legacy BYT 2310 · stub · DH`

A stretch of buried stone humming at a single, unchanging pitch, cold to the touch in every season. Three separate attempts to break through it are documented; each ended the same way — tools ruined, and within days every mason on the crew reporting the same headache that wouldn’t lift for a full turn of the moon, along with small, luminous vent sprites emerging from the cracked stone each time work began, agitated and unwilling to be driven off until the digging stopped. After the third attempt, the local clan-elders forbade further digging by decree — an Antem community legislating against curiosity outright, rather than simply losing interest in it the way every earlier such site had been left alone. The wall was built over deliberately, not forgotten, a distinction the record is careful to preserve even if the exact location wasn’t.

### Waiting in the Field
`Legacy BYT 2105 · stub · DH`

The full collapse of the Unmakings cult, remembered less as a tragedy than as one of Antem oral history’s favorite jokes at its own expense: the final Unmaking-date, publicly and confidently set decades earlier, arrived on an unremarkable morning, and nothing happened. No flood, no fire, no unmaking. The remaining faithful reportedly spent the morning waiting in a field, in their best clothes, and simply — eventually — went home. The sect never formally dissolved. It just stopped being taken seriously by anyone, including, within a generation, most of its own descendants. “Waiting in the field” survives today as an idiom for pointless certainty.


## The Age of Clans

*-12000 → -7000*

### Hawks Over Water
`Legacy BYT 1050 · c. 7,000 BY · stub · DH`

In the final years before the first Faerling refugee boats reached Faerlan’s shores, hunters across several unconnected coastal clans reported hawks refusing to fly out over open water at all — circling back the moment they crossed the shoreline, no matter how they were trained or pressed. No clan connected this to anything at the time. It’s remembered now, with the benefit of hindsight nobody had, as the closest thing to a warning the land ever gave before everything changed.

### The Bone Fields of Iowen-Ranu
`Legacy BYT 2040 · major · DH`

Within two generations of the Salt Line War’s exhausted peace, the abandoned battlefield itself became a problem neither clan had anticipated: skeletons and wights rose from the unburied dead in numbers no one had troubled to prevent, since neither Iowen’s nor Ranu’s descendants wanted to claim the ground long enough to properly bury the other side’s fallen. The two clans finally cooperated on something for the first time since the war itself — a joint, one-time burial expedition, grudging and silent, that cleared the worst of it. A handful of wights are still occasionally reported drifting the old flats. Nobody’s organized a second expedition since; the first one cost both sides more goodwill than either wanted to spend twice.

### The Treanted Wood of Greenreach
`Legacy BYT 1870 · stub · DH`

A stand of old forest that had stood undisturbed for as long as any clan’s memory reached began, over a single generation, to move — slow, deliberate, and only ever toward anyone attempting to clear it. Treants, unnamed in any Antem tongue at the time, ended three separate logging attempts by three separate clans before the wood was simply left alone by unanimous, unspoken local agreement. It remains uncut today.

### The Long Quarrel
`Legacy BYT 1740 · major · DH`

Not one war but four, fought across four generations between descendants of clans that could no longer articulate, by the final round, exactly what the original grievance had been. Each generation’s leaders inherited the fight along with the land, adding new grievances atop old ones nobody could untangle anymore — and each war left behind more unburied dead than the last, until ghouls became a standing hazard on both sides’ contested ground, scavenging battlefields faster than either clan’s dead could be properly honored. It ended, finally, not in resolution but in exhaustion so total that the last two clan-heads are recorded as having simply refused to fight a fifth time, regardless of what their advisors wanted — an Antem leader unilaterally ending an inherited war rather than passing it to the next generation, rather than the other way around.

### The Watcher’s Bargain
`Legacy BYT 1600 · stub · DH`

A clan besieged by a persistent poison dart sentinel nest near their water source struck an unlikely truce with it rather than continue losing hunters — leaving a portion of every kill at the nest’s edge in exchange for being left alone at the well. The arrangement held for two generations before the clan itself dissolved in the Scattering era, and no one since has dared test whether the sentinels still honor a bargain nobody living made.

### The Watchers of the High Stone
`Legacy BYT 1550 · major · DH`

The first recorded death attributed to the birds that would, millennia later, be named Cragwings: a young hunter named Ossel, testing his nerve against clan elders’ warnings, climbed toward a nesting shelf on a dare and was found at the cliff’s base that evening, the birds still circling above without ever having descended. Whether they killed him or simply startled him into falling was disputed even at the time — but the clan’s response wasn’t. Disturbing that nesting ground was made a banishable offense within the season, a taboo Antarkeia-era Antem would still be honoring in nearly identical terms three thousand years later.

### The Serpent Census
`Legacy BYT 1480 · stub · DH`

A minor but oddly precise piece of surviving record: a clan scribe’s tally of rattlesnake sightings across a single decade, rising sharply enough that the clan relocated its main camp half a day’s walk from its previous site. No cause is given. The tally itself is the only reason the relocation is remembered at all — a bureaucratic accident of record-keeping outliving the actual event it was tracking.

### Forty Days Unfed
`Legacy BYT 1390 · major · DH · CF`

The Long Fast Prophet’s actual ordeal, as best as it can be reconstructed — and it cannot be reconstructed cleanly. At least three incompatible versions survive: one in which he wandered the desert alone by choice, seeking answers; one in which he was cast out by his own clan and simply refused to die on schedule; one, favored by later hostile tellings, in which there was no fast at all, only a man who vanished for a season and returned with a story convenient enough to make him powerful. What all three agree on is that he came back changed, teaching that hunting demons was covenant rather than survival — and that whatever actually happened to him in that gap, nobody left alive can say with confidence which version, if any, is true.

### The Mudflat Dead
`Legacy BYT 1310 · stub · DH`

A river-adjacent clan lost eleven people over two seasons to what they initially blamed on drowning — until a survivor of the twelfth incident described being pulled under by something that gripped rather than merely swept. Mud lurkers, undocumented before this, are cited in every account since. The clan relocated its crossing point and never used the old ford again.

### The Second Prophet
`Legacy BYT 1240 · stub · DH`

A lesser-known figure attempted to repeat the Long Fast Prophet’s ordeal deliberately, walking into the same stretch of desert a generation later, hoping for the same authority. He never walked back out. A trader who found his abandoned camp two months on — bedroll, waterskins, a cookfire long cold, nothing touched or looted — spread the story that the man had simply given up and slunk home in shame. That version is the one that survives in most tellings, and it’s very likely a mercy rather than a fact: nothing about an untouched camp explains where a living man actually went.

### The Overturning at Sallowmere
`Legacy BYT 1150 · major · DH`

A rare documented instance of a Precursor-descended council — one of the last true holdouts of the old confederated governance style the Old Council had briefly modeled millennia earlier — being deposed not by outside conquest but by its own upstart clan leaders, tired of ceremonial authority with no teeth. The overthrow was reportedly bloodless; the deposed council simply found itself with no one left willing to enforce its rulings, and dissolved within a season. What replaced it was smaller, more local, and more heavily armed — a shift toward exactly the fragmented clan structure that would leave the Antem so vulnerable when both the Long Cold’s refugees and, later, the Hollowing arrived. Historians treat Sallowmere as the last gasp of centralized Antem governance before the modern era’s near-total absence of it.

### Iskrel Sixhold and the Unbroken Terms
`Late Age of Clans · vignette · NT 4 · CF`

A young Antem commander who conquers six rival holds in under a decade and dies young of an unnamed illness. His territory fractures on his death, but his arbitration norms — the Unbroken Terms — persist, filtered down through millennia of myth-drift into inherited Faerl legend and idiom.

### The Kindling Years
`The Scattering Wars · major · DH`

This is where the era genuinely earns the name Scattering Wars — not one conflict but several, breaking out among unconnected Antem clans within the same handful of years, none over the same grievance, none aware the others were happening. What made this stretch different from every earlier round of clan violence was what came after the fighting stopped: zombies, risen from battlefields too numerous and too scattered for any single clan to properly bury its own dead, began appearing in numbers that outpaced anything the era’s chroniclers had a framework for. No clan connected the wars to the rising dead at the time. In hindsight, the pattern is obvious — a world losing the discipline to honor its own losses was, without meaning to, teaching something worse how to use them.

### The Skirmish at Yalume Ford
`The Scattering Wars · stub · DH`

A minor engagement, forgettable in scale, between two Antem clans contesting a ford — except one of the two combatants was a young commander not yet called by the name history would remember him under. He won, barely, and is said to have kept the losing side’s banner as a personal trophy for the rest of his life. Later chroniclers, working backward from the Unbroken Terms, treat Yalume Ford as the first evidence of Iskrel Sixhold’s tactics — a man already fighting like someone who intended to stop fighting eventually.

### A Dead Man’s Leash
`The Scattering Wars · major · DH · NT 5`

Within five years of Iskrel Sixhold’s death, the largest of his former holds repudiated the Unbroken Terms outright, executing an arbitrator sent under its authority and declaring the dead commander’s peace “a dead man’s leash.” No other hold intervened. The Terms survived anyway — not because anyone enforced them, but because enough smaller clans, watching what naked repudiation had cost that hold in allies within a year, quietly kept honoring an agreement nobody could any longer compel them to. It is the clearest evidence Chronicle-Keepers have that the Unbroken Terms were never law. They were reputation, wearing law’s clothes, and reputation is what actually persisted.

### Kolek’s Manuscript — The Unmaking of Grael’s Promise
`Early Scattering Wars (frame) · vignette · VA`

Starving in the forests outside Antarkeia, the chieftain Kolek Tymtrove opens an iron cavern that shouldn’t exist — full of fresh food and a single manuscript: a parable about a promise broken one year too soon.


## The Sparse Contact Centuries

*-7000 → -5075*

### Ark 2 Arrives, and Scatters
`c. 7,000 BY · Genesis Yr 56,500 · major · RF · Ark 2 Addendum`

Twenty thousand years after the Crash, Ark 2 arrives into a world that has fully regressed from a devastated utopia into near-antiquity. It is a deportation vessel from a faction that didn’t care where it landed, and it scatters on arrival: small, disconnected pockets across several landmasses, avoiding Faerlan mostly by indifference, since Faerlan was the one continent with an established civilization worth steering clear of. Antarkeia was the good, deliberate landing. Ark 2 was the leftover nobody bothered aiming. Pockets on Kessara, the Vantry Isles and Kaeruvai are canon-legal and unexplored.

### Faerling and the Long Cold
`Ossmoor · major · DH §0`

One branch came down on Ossmoor, the cold northern continent — isolated and harsh enough that a small crew with minimal equipment could survive unnoticed by anyone on Faerlan. The exiles built a marginal coastal foothold they came to call Faerling — not a nation, just the name of the strip of coast that kept them alive. “Faerl” is the ethnonym that outlived the place-name, the way real peoples end up named for a homeland nobody living has seen.

What pushed them south was a recurring climate cycle they called the Long Cold — a punishing stretch of hard winters every few centuries that squeezed an already marginal coast past what it could support. The decisive cycle split Faerling into rival factions fighting over what little arable land remained — the first real Faerl infighting on record, and the ancestor pattern the four paramount houses would later repeat at a much larger scale. The losing faction took to boats and drifted south along the coast, generation replacing generation aboard vessels never meant for a voyage this long.

### The Long Drift
`Unmarked · stub · DH`

Somewhere in the unrecorded span between Ark 2’s landing and the oldest Antem memory of them, the exiles who would become the Faerl did not vanish — they simply stopped mattering to anyone keeping a record. Scattered, few, and without the tools their ancestors had once had, they survived in small pockets far from Antem-dominated ground, leaving nothing behind that any later chronicler could date. When Faerl finally appear in any Antem account, no one on either side has any memory that this is a reunion rather than a first meeting.

### The First Watching
`Sparse Contact · major · DH · NT 6`

Antem hunters working the northern coast reported the newcomers for months before any contact was made — small, wary camps that broke and moved often, never staying anywhere long enough to be worth approaching. The earliest Antem word for them, in the trade-pidgin that would later develop, translates roughly to “the driftwood people” — not unkind, just accurate. Nobody at the time imagined the driftwood people would still be here millennia later, let alone that their descendants would eventually rename the whole of Vaelume after themselves.

### Vaelume, the Unforgetting Ground
`Sparse Contact · stub · DH §0`

The Antem were never one polity long enough to need a single name for the whole continent. But one old name survives, poetic rather than political: in the oldest surviving Long Grief liturgy, the whole land is called Vaelume — “the Unforgetting Ground,” the one word every Antem clan tradition seems to agree on independently. “Faerlan” as the name of the whole landmass is a Faerl imposition — only a people trying to claim it as one thing would need to name it as one thing.

### The Trade at Elder’s Reach
`Sparse Contact · stub · DH`

Antem and Faerl exchanging goods, rather than simply avoiding one another, on record for the first time — a small clan, curious rather than hostile, traded dried fish and woven cloth for a handful of Faerl-forged iron tools the clan had never seen the like of before. The exchange repeated itself a handful more times over the following years, never formalized, never guaranteed, and stopped entirely within a generation as both sides drifted back out of each other’s range. Chronicle-Keepers still cite Elder’s Reach as proof that hostility wasn’t the only possible outcome of contact this early — even if, in the end, it was never given room to become anything more than this one brief exchange.

### The Grain Winter
`Sparse Contact · stub · DH`

A multi-year cold snap devastated both Antem grazing land and a scattering of fragile early Faerl camps simultaneously — the first crisis to hit both peoples at once rather than one and not the other. It produced what’s remembered now as this whole span’s single friendliest moment: a couple of documented instances of an Antem clan sharing stores with a nearby Faerl camp that had nothing to trade in return, and one joint effort to clear a hound den threatening both. It was brief, isolated, and almost entirely forgotten by both descendant cultures within a few centuries, buried under everything that came after.

### The Toll of the Green Road
`Sparse Contact · stub · DH`

Faerl loggers pushing a small timber claim into forest fringe still nominally Antem ground found the work slower than expected — goblin raiding parties harassed the work for the better part of a decade before the loggers began leaving a portion of felled timber at the forest edge as an unofficial toll. It worked. The practice is the quiet, long-forgotten ancestor of a modern custom or two, its actual origin lost along with everyone who knew it.

### The Return at Spearpoint
`Sparse Contact · stub · DH`

A small Faerl party, unfamiliar with Antem mourning custom and desperate for anything valuable to trade through a lean season, broke into a sealed Antem ancestor-ground and stripped it of grave goods before any clan discovered the intrusion. The offended clan wanted nothing but the items returned, which they were, in full, within the month. It’s a rare instance of a group of Faerl policing their own specifically to avoid provoking a neighbor they had every reason to fear.

### The Elmsworth Ford Incident
`Sparse Contact · stub · DH`

An armed skirmish between Antem and Faerl — the earliest one on any surviving record, small and forgettable by the standards of what would eventually come, a handful dead on each side, fought over a river crossing neither side had previously bothered to contest. Both sides, by surviving account, seemed genuinely startled it had happened at all, and the crossing was left mutually unused for generations afterward.

### The Ridgeway Party’s Toll
`Sparse Contact · stub · DH`

A small Faerl party, ambitious and poorly prepared, attempted to cross a mountain pass into highland territory they didn’t know. They didn’t account for what Antem hunters in the region already avoided by long-standing, unspoken rule: a resident storm caller whose presence reliably preceded violent, localized weather no ordinary forecasting could predict. Fewer than half made it back down.

### The Boiling of Lake Ilmere
`Sparse Contact · stub · DH`

Without warning, over a single night, a lake supporting a handful of scattered Faerl camps and an Antem fishing clan rose to a temperature that killed nearly everything living in it. Both peoples abandoned the lakeshore within the year and never fully resettled it — one more incident that ends in both sides simply walking away from each other rather than toward anything.


## The Hollowing, the Rivening & Fragmentation

*-5075 → -5000*

### Ilvane’s Unfinished Warning
`c. 5,075 BY · stub · HR`

A wandering scholar named Ilvane, who had spent years cataloguing sealed and half-buried ruin-ground across the fringe of what would later be Antarkeia’s contested ground, sent word ahead to at least two settlements roughly three years before the plague, warning them off a specific stretch she’d marked as “wrong in a way I don’t have the words for yet” — ground she also noted as drawing heavier wraith activity than anything else she’d surveyed. One letter was lost; the other survives only as a secondhand paraphrase by someone who admitted he hadn’t taken it seriously. It was this exact stretch of ground that was broken into three years later. Ilvane is not recorded as dying in the plague or surviving it. She simply isn’t mentioned again.

### The Hollowing’s Origin Trigger
`c. 5,072 BY · vignette · HR · NT 10`

A Faerl handler, traveling a border route with Antem travelers of unresolved status, breaks into — or accidentally triggers — something sealed in old ruin-ground. Within a season, people start dying who have no business dying. Who bears responsibility is deliberately never resolved.

### The Spread and the Asymmetry
`c. 5,070 – 5,050 BY · major · HR`

The sickness did not arrive once and pass. It came in waves, recurring across roughly two decades, which mattered more than any single bad year could have — a catastrophe that keeps returning invites blame in a way a single disaster doesn’t. It struck the Antem hardest by an overwhelming margin. Faerl populations were almost entirely untouched — a stark, total split with no partial or in-between cases, which is part of what makes it so hard to write off as ordinary spread. Nobody living through it could explain why. Nobody living now can either.

### The Two Names, and the Unhollowed
`Plague years · stub · HR`

The Faerl name is simply “the Hollowing” — clinical, general, and silent on whose plague it actually was; less a description of the disease than of Faerl distance from it. The Antem memory is more physical — bodies, homes and clan-halls emptied out from the inside — and never settles on a single term. Faerl who lived through it untouched, or could later claim ancestry unmarked by it, began calling themselves the Unhollowed: not yet a doctrine, just an identity born of relief. That word is the seed of the Proven Right.

### The Legend of the Weapon
`Plague years · stub · HR`

Because the sickness spared one people and gutted another so completely, and because it reportedly began with something sealed and ancient disturbed near the site, a persistent legend holds that the Hollowing was design, not accident — a lost bioweapon shipped to this world by unknown hands, either lost before it could be used or deployed exactly as intended. It sits beside two competing tellings: pure accident, and an uglier, more common one that blames the Antem for bringing it on themselves. All three coexist. None resolve.

### The Sealed Season at Twin Hearth
`Second wave · stub · HR`

As the plague’s second wave broke over the interior, a small mixed settlement of Antem and Faerl households — an unusually close-living arrangement for the era — made a decision no larger power ever ordered and no chronicle credits anyone by name for: they sealed their own boundary, admitted no one, and shared what stores they had evenly, through the better part of a year that no neighboring settlement matched for survival rate. It should be one of the era’s few genuinely hopeful stories. It isn’t remembered that way. Because the settlement survived largely intact while others around it didn’t, later Proven Right doctrine quietly recast Twin Hearth’s discipline as suspicious — proof, to those looking for it, that the settlement had known something, or done something, the plague itself was sparing them for.

### The Last Light of House Verrin
`Plague years · vignette · HR · NT 11`

A fading Faerl noble line stakes everything on a scavenged “blessed hearthstone,” warm without fuel. Three winters it keeps them alive. On the fourth, the hall and all twelve people in it are gone by morning — floor glassed smooth, walls folded outward. Masons still call such floors “verrin-glass.”

### The Half-Word at Dresh
`Worst season · stub · HR`

The single closest thing to a demon sighting the Hollowing produced. A man named Corrin Dresh, found dying two days after his entire hunting party vanished during the plague’s worst season, managed one broken sentence before he died: “it wasn’t red like blood, it was red like—” and nothing after. The phrase “red like Dresh” entered Faerl speech within a generation as shorthand for a fear too specific to actually name, its origin already half-forgotten by the people using it.

### Backward Over Plague Ground
`Worst spread · stub · HR`

A single Cragwing, sighted repeatedly over the same stretch of plague-emptied ground during the Hollowing’s worst spread, was reported by multiple unconnected witnesses to fly its circling pattern in reverse of every other known sighting. No consequence was ever attributed to it beyond the report itself.

### The Departure of the Stillwings
`c. 5,055 – 5,046 BY · vignette · HR · NT 12`

As riders die, sightings thin — the bond was grief-deep, closer to kinship than domestication. Around 5,046 BY the last of them leave all at once, in a single visible departure, read as the worst omen anyone alive had witnessed. Where they went is unconfirmed; legend points to Kaeruvai.

### The Great Rivening
`c. 5,045 – 5,025 BY · major · HR`

By the time the worst plague waves had passed, the Antem were left without their most vulnerable dead, without any means of surveying or defending their land from above, and without a single voice able to hold what remained together. What followed — a roughly twenty-year internal war — was not fought over ideology. It was fought over what was left: land, herds, grazing rights, blame for two decades of unexplained death, and the vacuum where flight and the status it carried used to sit. The war didn’t cause the Stillwings’ loss. It made sure no clan that came out of the fighting was ever strong enough to matter again.

### Colehurst’s War Debt
`During the Rivening · stub · HR`

As the Rivening tore Antem clan structure apart, House Colehurst extended forge-credit to Antem refugee groups desperate for weapons and tools they no longer had smiths to make — the same debt-as-leverage pattern the house had already perfected on Faerl smallholds generations earlier, offered now to a people with nothing left to negotiate with. Every debt was collected in land, labor, or loyalty within a generation. By the Rivening’s end, Colehurst held more contested former-Antem ground than any house had acquired by outright conquest in the era, without a single Colehurst banner having marched to take it.

### The Sentinel Who Stayed
`During the Rivening · stub · HR`

A folk-story, likely composed well after the fact, describing a lone Antem sentinel who kept a post through the Rivening’s worst fighting long after the clan he guarded had scattered or died, refusing to abandon a wall nobody was left to defend. No name survives for him.

### Dossa’s Field
`During the Rivening · major · HR`

Two Antem clans — kin by marriage a generation earlier, allies until the Rivening turned every alliance provisional — met at Dossa’s Field over grazing rights neither could any longer afford to concede. What followed wasn’t a battle so much as a slaughter with no clear winner; both clans lost enough fighting strength that neither survived as an independent body past the decade. Antem oral history remembers the field less for the dead than for what one of the two clan-heads is recorded saying before the fighting started, to a kinsman-by-marriage he was about to kill: “We are already the last of what we were. I would rather we both be finished than one of us live to forget the other existed.” Both got their wish.

### The Words That Outlived Aldren Wyke
`Rivening · Aldren Wyke · major · HR`

What began during the plague years as scattered relief — we were spared, and they were not — calcified over a generation into genuine doctrine: the Proven Right, the conviction that the Hollowing’s asymmetry wasn’t luck but sanction. One name survives where most don’t: a settler named Aldren Wyke, whose address to a besieged Faerl camp during the worst of the Rivening survives nearly whole — “We did not choose to be spared. We were chosen. And a people chosen to live does not owe the dead an apology for outliving them.” It wasn’t the first such claim. It was the first said plainly and confidently enough that people stopped treating it as comfort and started treating it as fact. Wyke died within the year, forgotten almost at once. The sentence has outlived him by five thousand years.

### The Vessels’ Long Decline
`c. 5,025 – 5,000 BY · stub · HR`

The Vessels’ disappearance was not a separate catastrophe — it was the same slow bleed that took the Antem, on the same timeline, for the same underlying reason. Fewer Antem clans meant fewer bonds, fewer hands for whatever maintenance and knowledge-transfer had kept generations of Vessels functioning, entire lineages of the bond gone quietly in the plague and the war. When the last organized Antem clans died out, there were no Vessels left to outlive them. The bond, and the people who carried it, ended together — thousands of years before any Faerl coalition reached Antarkeia.

### The Blight of the Standing Grove
`Fragmentation · stub · HR`

A stand of old-growth forest on contested former-Antem ground died standing over a single season — not burned, not felled, simply gone grey and hollow trunk by trunk, spreading outward from a single point rather than sweeping through as blight or fire would. A scorched treant, the first of its kind anyone had documented, was found rooted dead-center of the blight. Nothing has grown properly in the blighted patch since.

### The Boundary Stone at Wickfall
`Fragmentation · stub · HR`

Thorne and Dray, both expanding hard into ground the collapsing Antem clans could no longer hold, nearly went to open war over a boundary stone neither house could prove had been placed by their own ancestors rather than the other’s. The dispute was settled not by combat but by a Marlow-brokered arbitration — one of the earliest recorded instances of any paramount house successfully mediating between two others. The stone was never moved. It still marks the boundary today.

### The Wraiths Draw Closer
`Fragmentation · stub · HR`

In the final years of any real Antem presence near Antarkeia’s old gathering-grounds, before the last of them scattered or died, Antem hunters working the fringes there began reporting the drifting wraiths of that ground — long treated as passive, ambient, simply there — moving with what several accounts independently described as purpose, following rather than merely haunting. No attack was ever recorded. No explanation survived with the people who might have offered one.

### Fragmentation’s Final Days
`c. 5,000 BY · vignette · HR · NT 13`

The last Antem die not in a collapse but in the quiet way a fire goes out when nobody is left to feed it — clan-sized, then family-sized, then individuals. The last Vessels go with them. Antarkeia’s automated containment keeps running without anyone to tend it, for five thousand years.


## The Landfall Centuries

*-5150 → -4000*

### Landfall on a Borrowed Shore
`c. 5,150 BY · vignette · DH · NT 8`

The losing faction of Faerling’s worst Long Cold split, generations at sea, finally makes landfall on Vaelume’s shore — not an invading force, but survivors of survivors, carrying nothing but each other and a name for a homeland none of them had seen.

### The First Forge-Debt
`c. 5,100 BY · major · DH`

A minor Colehurst ancestor, holding little but the coal his own house-founder had kept unexplained for generations, began lending fire and forge-labor to neighboring smallholds in exchange for a cut of every harvest those forges helped produce — not conquest, just leverage, offered freely and repaid, at first, gladly. Within thirty years, every forge within two days’ ride owed Colehurst something. It’s the earliest surviving example of the pattern that would eventually define the house entirely: reach offered as generosity, and collected later as obligation.

### The Unnamed Mother Church, and the Quiet Split
`c. 5,000 BY · major · DH · CF`

Early in the Faerl Age a mother church takes shape, teaching that demons exist to test the faithful. Within a decade of its founding, a handful of dissenting voices broke from it, preaching instead that demons were simply hungry and mindless. The breakaway vanishes from record within two generations. Chronicle-Keepers today consider it the earliest attested ancestor of their own unromantic tradition. The mother church itself survives until the Consolidated Faith’s power-grab schism in YT 95.

### The Betrayal at Greymoot
`c. 4,700 BY · major · FE 46`

An early Faerl stronghold, larger and better-defended than anything else built in that era, falls in a single night when one of its own gatekeepers opens a side passage for a rival camp he’d secretly been trading with for years. The stronghold’s survivors scatter rather than rebuild, and the era’s already-thin trust in permanent settlement hardens into active avoidance — the deep, mostly-forgotten root of why Farhand culture in particular never builds anything meant to last.

### The First Waterskin
`Landfall Centuries · stub · FE 1`

A Landfall-era camp survives a three-season drought only because one family refused to share their reserve — reviled at the time, credited generations later as the unspoken root of Dray’s entire water-discipline culture, long before there was a House Dray to claim it.

### The Sand That Swallowed the Second Camp
`Landfall Centuries · stub · DH`

A group of Faerl, pushing inland in search of ground the coast hadn’t given them, made camp on what looked like firm desert flats and lost fourteen people in a single night to a den of sand serpents disturbed by their digging. The handful who made it back carried a lesson the rest of the Faerl took seriously for generations: the interior wasn’t empty ground waiting to be claimed.

### The Scarab Wardens
`Landfall Centuries · stub · FE 2`

Early Faerl settlers near the desert’s edge notice that graves left undisturbed by scavengers all share one thing: an infestation of oversized, territorial beetles nesting nearby. They begin deliberately encouraging the nests near burial ground. [Guardian Scarab]

### The Buried Roof
`Landfall Centuries · stub · DH`

Early Faerl settlers built a central longhouse directly atop what they took for bedrock, without digging further. In the third generation, the ground gave way in a single night, swallowing four sleepers into a pit a sinkhole lurker had apparently been living beneath the whole time. The settlement relocated and never spoke of why.

### The Quiet Threshold
`Landfall Centuries · stub · FE 3`

A Farhand family is the first on record to refuse entry to their dead relative’s return — a superstition against welcoming anything back through a door it had already left through. The custom outlives the family by millennia, now just “don’t answer a third knock.”

### The Salt Road’s First Toll
`Landfall Centuries · stub · FE 4`

Two unrelated camps meet at a natural salt-flat crossing and, rather than fight over it, invent the concept of a toll — paid in meat, tools, or labor. Neither camp remembers inventing anything; they just remember it was always done that way.

### The Long Bark
`Landfall Centuries · stub · FE 5`

Faerl herders first notice a wild canine that howls only at dusk, in a pattern too structured to be random — three rising notes, always the same three. They start listening for it as a storm-warning. It’s right often enough to matter. [Dogkin]

### The Vault of House Oswick
`Landfall Centuries · stub · DH`

An early, minor Faerl proto-house — never one of the four that would eventually matter, extinct within a single generation — built its entire claim to authority around a sealed chamber its founder discovered and never opened, only displayed. His son, less patient, opened it. What happened next left no bodies to bury and no chamber left standing. The name Oswick survives solely as a regional curse-word for greed that outreaches its own understanding.

### Ossin Walks the Debt
`Landfall Centuries · stub · DH`

A Farhand guide led a merchant party safely through dangerous desert ground and was refused his fee at the far end. He’s said to have walked back into the desert alone and cursed the route rather than the men who’d wronged him. The route acquired a documented, unusually high rate of traveler deaths for two centuries afterward. A disputed folk-claim calls it “a lesser cousin of the White Wind, minted local.”

### The White Wind
`Landfall Centuries · the Glass Plains · vignette · CF · NT 7`

Renna Chases the Mirage, a Farhand guide leading refugees across the Glass Plains, meets a sourceless bone-white wind that drains the color from everything it touches. Three of her party vanish. She walks out unmarked, fearless in an unsettling way, and never speaks of them. “Walked through the White Wind” survives, detached from her name.


## The Warlord Centuries

*-4000 → -3200*

### The Emptying of Hallow’s Bend
`Early Warlord Centuries · major · DH`

A border village of some forty Faerl was found entirely without its people — homes standing, meals left mid-preparation, every dyed cloth and painted door gone the same washed-grey nothing-color. Nothing was ever recovered. “Gone grey as Hallow’s Bend” still describes anything drained past the point of ordinary loss.

### The Waking at Drossglen
`c. 3,600 BY · major · FE 47`

A warlord, having recovered a relic core intact enough to still respond to touch, deliberately tries to wield it as a weapon against a rival camp rather than simply carry it as loot. Whatever it does instead kills his own warband and leaves a crater at Drossglen still faintly warm today. No formal law follows — none is needed. “Don’t wake what’s sleeping” becomes not superstition but absolute, near-universal practice — the real reason nobody in this world experiments with relics.

### The Siege of Emberfall
`Late Warlord Centuries · major · MS · Part 3 placement`

A coastal city besieged nearly a year over a stolen betrothal-relic nobody remembers anymore. Every surviving version of the song about it ends differently. It is the culture’s founding epic, sung long after — this world’s Troy. (A different Emberfall event from the Refusal at Emberfall on the eve of the Breach.)

### The Ninefinger Reckoning
`Warlord Centuries · stub · FE 6`

A warlord missing four fingers from a training accident builds a reputation on being harder to kill than a whole man — eventually giving his diminished name to what becomes a Dray vassal hold (Ninefinger).

### The Concordance of Ash
`Warlord Centuries · major · MS · Part 3 placement`

Merchants, not scholars, forced a common script into being to standardize trade ledgers — now taught as high culture. It is the precondition for everything that follows: the Gathering Crown will later build an empire on it.

### The Marsh Accord
`Warlord Centuries · stub · FE 7`

Two rival warlords, unable to take a contested wetland by force, agree to alternate control season by season. It works for six generations before anyone remembers why, then collapses the first year someone forgets to hand it back on schedule.

### The Weeping Wolves
`Warlord Centuries · stub · FE 8`

A Faerl hunting band, cornered by an unusually large wolf pack, survives only because the pack breaks off the attack at the sound of a horn — a domesticated-stock instinct nobody alive understands, credited instead to a local storm-spirit. [Wolf]

### The Warlord Who Counted Wrong
`Warlord Centuries · stub · FE 9`

A petty chieftain, convinced by a miscounted census that his warband outnumbered a rival’s two to one, attacks and is annihilated. “Counted wrong as [his name]” becomes shorthand for confident, fatal error — the name itself lost to time, the idiom outliving it.

### The First Roof-Oath
`Warlord Centuries · stub · FE 10`

A warband leader, tired of losing men to petty desertion, institutes the practice of publicly swearing loyalty under a literal roof rather than open sky — the deep, unremembered root of what becomes the Shared Roof institution three thousand years later.

### The Ash-Grey Herd
`Warlord Centuries · stub · FE 11`

A Faerl scout reports cattle near a demon-scarred field turning the same washed-grey color as Hallow’s Bend’s missing villagers — except the cattle are still alive, still grazing, apparently unbothered. Nobody ever explains it. The herd is left alone. [Pale Cattle]

### The Raising of the Sundered Spire
`Warlord Centuries · stub · MS · Part 3 placement`

A forgettable petty-king conscripts golem-labor to raise a record tower. It cracked down the center the winter it finished and still stands, sundered but unfallen.

### The Warlord’s Debt to No One
`Warlord Centuries · vignette · FE 12 · NT 9`

A rare warlord who refuses all forge-debt entanglements, paying cash or labor for everything, dies wealthy and unallied — his hold absorbed within a decade by neighbors who’d extended him no favors to call in. The lesson later chroniclers draw: independence and isolation cost the same price.

### The Naming of the Groundcallers
`Warlord Centuries · stub · FE 13`

Faerl settlers first document a burrowing creature that seems to respond to rhythmic stomping — used deliberately, generations later, to flush game or warn off worse things. [Groundcallers]

### Highbrand’s Warning
`Warlord Centuries · stub · DH`

A small band of Faerl prospectors, pushing west toward the smoldering edge of the continent near the Great Vantry Rift’s mainland shadow, found ground warm enough to work metal without a forge of their own — and found, too late, that the warmth wasn’t geological. Ember spirits killed most of the band within the season. The ground itself, generations later, is exactly the ground House Colehurst would eventually build its fire-forges beside.


## The Widow’s War

*-3200 → -3050*

### The Widow’s Accords
`c. 3,200 – 3,100 BY · vignette · VA`

After decades of war take nearly eighty percent of the continent’s war-ready men, a council of one hundred women — the Widows — builds a matriarchal peace that holds for some fifty years, until Beghar Flintbrow comes back from the desert with amber in his eyes.

### The Unweighed Grain
`Famine years · stub · FE 14`

During the worst of the war’s famine years, a single granary town refuses to hoard against its neighbors, distributing stores freely even as its own people go hungry. It survives, barely, and becomes the model the Widow’s Accords later cites when arguing for shared reserves.

### The Last Warlord’s Bargain
`Widow’s War · stub · FE 15`

A minor warlord, facing certain defeat, offers his own sons as hostages in exchange for his people’s safety — an early precedent the Widows draw on when structuring their own council’s authority over surviving men.

### Beghar’s First Lie
`Years before the revolt · stub · FE 16`

Years before his revolt, Beghar Flintbrow is recorded telling a border patrol he’d “seen nothing” in the desert he’d just returned from — already lying, already changed, and nobody thought to ask twice. The line “seen nothing, like Beghar” becomes an idiom for a lie so smooth it isn’t even suspected.


## The Charter Centuries

*-3050 → -2800*

### The Charter of Salt and Silence
`c. a generation after the Accords fall · vignette · VA`

House Dravencourt stakes everything on one ship, the Second Chance, and forty debtors chasing the Vantry Isles. Nineteen years later a single man comes back, in a boat he can’t name. The House is absorbed within the decade; “a Dravencourt charter” still means a deal only a desperate man would sign.

### The Widow’s Blush Toll-Keepers
`Charter Centuries · stub · FE 17`

The first formal wardens of the preserved forest trade route are appointed — a role that becomes hereditary, then ceremonial, then, within two centuries, entirely forgotten as anything but a place-name.

### The Second Charter, Unfunded
`After Dravencourt · stub · FE 19`

In the wake of House Dravencourt’s collapse, a rival coastal warlord proposes a second Vantry expedition — and can’t raise a single backer. No one sails again for generations. The refusal itself becomes a minor legend: “wiser than Dravencourt, or just poorer.”

### The Drowning Choir
`Charter Centuries · stub · FE 18`

Sailors along the coast begin reporting a sound like birds crying just beneath the waterline before a storm — real, native, and eventually used as an informal weather-warning by anyone willing to wade out and listen. [Drowning Birds]

### The Boy Who Counted Stars
`Charter Centuries · vignette · VA`

Flynn Ofshalleiu, the paralyzed only son of a warlord, is set out nightly to read the sky — and becomes the most influential mind the Faerl ever produce, the first to see and triangulate the Black Mun. All modern seafaring and astrology rests on his observations.

### The Mirror Deer Cull
`Charter Centuries · stub · FE 20`

A Faerl hunting tradition develops around a deer whose antlers grow in near-perfect bilateral symmetry — prized, hunted almost to local extinction within a generation, then protected by informal taboo once someone notices how rare the symmetry actually was. [Mirror Deer]

### A Drifting Coastline
`Drifting texture, Charter Centuries onward · stub · DH`

Faerl explorers reported an island that appeared on two separate expeditions’ charts in slightly different positions each time — an island drifter, unrecognized as a living thing at the time. The coastline remains under-mapped to this day, more from lingering unease than real difficulty.

### The Buried Song
`Drifting texture · stub · DH`

Faerl herders reported, for two seasons, a faint rhythmic sound from deep within a cave system — “a machine’s lullaby,” by one account, though the teller had no framework for what a machine was. The cave was sealed by a rockslide the following winter and never reopened.

### Aldermoot’s Circling Bird, and the Ash Vigil
`Drifting texture · stub · DH · CF`

A single Cragwing circled a Faerl settlement for the better part of a day without touching down, then flew off and was never seen again. Within the decade, that same settlement produced the earliest teachers of the Ash Vigil — Faerl settlers absorbing a garbled echo of the long-dead Antem Long Grief — who pointed backward at the bird as the first sign the land had taken notice of them. The Vigil fades much later, as the Reckoning Faith and the Consolidated Faith absorb its communities.

### The Tally of the Nine Ledgers
`Drifting texture · stub · DH`

Nine Faerl trading families, scattered and unconnected, are recorded independently adopting the same counting method within a few years of each other — a coincidence with no agreed explanation. It became the standard reckoning still taught to merchant apprentices today.

### The Warding of the Hollow Root
`Drifting texture · stub · DH`

A Thorne-adjacent clan, unable to clear a hedge hound den, began leaving meat at the base of the tree each equinox instead. It worked well enough that the practice outlived both the clan and the den, absorbed wholesale into modern Thorne vassal custom as simple reverence, the original fear long forgotten.

### The Comb of Bone
`Drifting texture · stub · DH`

A grave-good recovered from an unmarked burial — a comb carved from something that wasn’t bone, in a script no scholar since has been able to place. The one researcher who tried seriously, Colehurst-sponsored, stopped publishing abruptly, leaving notes describing a recurring headache and a conviction the script “wasn’t meant to be read by anyone who’d still want to read it after.” The comb sits uncatalogued in a private Colehurst collection today.


## The Gathering Crown

*-2800 → -1800*

### Verah Kestrel and the Founding of the Gathering Crown
`c. 2,800 BY · vignette · FE 21 · NT 16`

Before founding the Crown, Verah is offered marriage into an already-powerful clan that would have given her authority without a fight. She refuses it, insisting on earning the claim instead — the founding myth’s most-repeated detail, whether or not it happened exactly that way.

### The Common Ledger
`Gathering Crown · stub · FE 22`

Kestrel’s Crown formalizes the Concordance of Ash script into an empire-wide tax and trade record — the first time in Faerl history anyone can prove, on paper, what they’re owed.

### The Nearhorse Levy
`Gathering Crown · stub · FE 23`

The Crown standardizes mounted messenger service using a smaller, hardier breed better suited to long relay routes than warhorses — the origin of every later “royal road” network the Houses inherit and maintain without knowing who built it. [Nearhorses]

### The Nine Governors’ Oath
`Gathering Crown · stub · FE 24`

The Crown divides its territory among nine appointed governors, sworn directly to the throne rather than to each other — a structure explicitly designed to prevent exactly the kind of regional consolidation that later becomes the four Houses. It works for six generations.

### The Writ Before the Writ
`c. 1,900 BY · stub · FE 27`

A full century before Tamrin Kestrel is forced to sign away central power, an earlier heir voluntarily delegates tax authority to regional governors during a succession crisis — a precedent Tamrin’s opponents cite directly as proof the Crown had already conceded the principle once.

### The Writ of Tamrin
`c. 1,800 BY · vignette · NT 17`

All nine governors move at once and force the last Kestrel heir to sign away central enforcement in exchange for keeping the crown in name. No battle — a document. “Provincial rights” becomes a citable legal idea every later House traces its claim back to.

### The Quiet Purge
`Gathering Crown · stub · FE 25`

A Kestrel-line heir, suspicious of a governor’s growing popularity, has him quietly removed rather than publicly tried — the Crown’s only well-documented act of court violence, and the first crack in its reputation for even-handed rule.

### The Laughing Pack’s Warning
`Gathering Crown · stub · FE 26`

Crown border-scouts document a pack predator whose calls mimic human laughter closely enough to lure the unwary — first dismissed as folk exaggeration, then confirmed, then simply avoided. [Laughing Pack]


## The Long Fracture

*-1800 → -1000*

### The Governors Who Kept the Roads
`After the Writ · stub · FE 28`

In the decades after the Writ of Tamrin, most of the nine governorships collapse into local warlordism — except three, which keep maintaining the old royal road network out of simple habit, not loyalty. Those three roads are the ones still usable, in fragments, in the present day.

### The Second Crossing
`c. 1,500 BY · vignette · FE 30 · NT 15`

### The Refusal at Emberfall
`Eve of the march · major · CA`

The last recorded attempt at a negotiated approach before the four houses marched: representatives of Thorne, Dray, and Colehurst met at Emberfall to discuss dividing whatever the expedition found without turning on each other over it, and were joined, unexpectedly, by a Marlow envoy proposing something none of the other three wanted to hear — that whatever remained of the Antem, in relic, ruin, or grave-good, be formally preserved and catalogued rather than simply divided and carried off. The other three refused outright, not out of any particular malice the record bothers preserving, but out of simple arithmetic: a formal preservation claim meant less for everyone else to take. The envoy was sent home. Marlow’s own river-lords, delayed at a silted crossing during the march itself, would arrive at Antarkeia last of all four banners — a coincidence no one in the moment connected to the table they’d been turned away from, though at least one later Marlow chronicler couldn’t resist noting that arriving late to matter, for this house, was becoming something close to tradition.

A minor Thorne vassal hall holds a formal seat at its high table, set and served at every feast, for a guest who never arrived and was never named. The custom persists to this day. No one currently serving there can say who it was originally set for.

A Faerl conscript, sworn to no house in particular, who held a narrow chokepoint alone for an afternoon after the rest of her company was lost to a passage collapse — armed with a spear that reportedly wasn’t hers, and had no business being anywhere near the surface world before that day. Author of the single most-cited account of Antarkeia’s final days, who admits in his own closing lines that he arrived four days after the breach ended and reconstructed everything from survivors. His name is lost; only the account remains.

### The Boy Who Lived to Tell It
`YT 4 · major · DH`

The border hamlet of Thistleford was found in ruin four days after a traveling tinker last passed through it whole — timber charred in patterns that matched no fire anyone could reconstruct, a dozen dead where two dozen had lived, and one survivor: a boy named Renn Coldwell, alive because he’d been hiding from a scolding when it happened. What he described — a shape the wrong color for anything that should burn, moving through the wreckage without ever quite touching it — was, for the first time in the world’s memory, believed. Not because his account was clean; it wasn’t, and never fully cohered. It was believed because everyone alive still remembered Antarkeia, four years gone, and Cael Thorncrest’s stopped count, and understood, collectively, that the age of nobody surviving to describe it had just ended. `YT 9 · stub · DH`

### Duskmoor Wood
`YT 14 · major · DH`

Two woodcutters who went in partway through to investigate didn’t come back. What did come back, near the end, was the color itself — every dyed cloth and painted door in the nearest village losing its hue overnight, in a pattern the older among them recognized immediately from stories out of the Hollowing era, though none of them had a word for what they were recognizing. The Bound’s earliest formal chapter records list Duskmoor Wood as their second confirmed demon incident, and the first one anyone thought to write down while it was still happening rather than after.

`YT c. 15 – 30 · major · HR · CF`

The devotional twin of the Boneyard, organizing demon-hunting as obligation rather than trade. “I am one of the Bound.” The name deliberately carries two readings at once: selfishly, an inheritance is something given — the demon-haunted ground the Antem once held now belongs to the Unhollowed by right; selflessly, an inheritance comes with obligations — the debt of tending what was left behind, the duty to prune the garden the Antem no longer can. `YT 24 · major · DH`

Survivors from both holdings gave up the well entirely rather than ever contest it again. ### The Faces at Millstead
`YT 33 · major · DH`

A trade town of some two hundred people simply stopped answering correspondence, and the merchant caravan sent to investigate three weeks later found the gates standing open and nobody left inside who could still be called anybody. ### The Historian Who Wrote It Twice
`YT 38 · major · DH`

No forger was ever caught, no motive established, and the older archivists on staff swore, with visible unease, that neither version was the one they remembered writing. ### The Guest Who Wore Her Face
`YT 87 · major · DH`

A minor noble estate maintained, for reasons its own family had long since forgotten, a chamber with two facing mirrors set an exact, deliberate distance apart — an old custom, harmless as far as anyone knew, until the family’s youngest daughter stepped between them on a dare and something stepped back out that answered to her name, wore her face, and knew things about her that only she should have known. What became of either the girl or the thing that replaced her isn’t recorded past that point — the family sealed the chamber and never spoke of it again, and it passed into regional legend as the Room of Two Mirrors, still occasionally rumored to exist somewhere, its actual location lost along with whichever family it belonged to.

### Locked, Not Lost
`YT 92 · major · DH`

House Latchpit, whose own motto had always been “locked, not lost,” held for two generations a sealed cylinder recovered from ruin-ground during the family’s founding — kept exactly as found, out of what the family itself described as prudence rather than fear. The second generation’s heir, ambitious and unwilling to inherit only caution, had it forced open by a hired locksmith against his own father’s dying wish. What followed left the family seat a crater and killed everyone inside it, the locksmith included, in a flash bright enough to be seen from two neighboring vassal halls. House Latchpit’s motto survives its own extinction as regional irony — “locked, not lost” now said, dryly, of anyone who insists on opening something they were explicitly warned not to.

### The Consolidated Faith
`YT 95 · stub · CF`

A power-grab schism off the old unnamed mother church, some five thousand years after that church’s founding. It becomes the dominant state religion of the present — and the furthest of any faith from the truth.

### The Smoke Year War and the Forgehand Rising
`YT 95 – 103 · vignette · CF · NT 24 · MS`

Aldous Colehurst’s Dominion — briefly more land than any House had held since Consolidation — overreaches on conscription and forge-tithes. Three vassal houses break; eight years of war rain real ash on the Fire zone; and the capital falls from inside, to a low-born forge-hand named Garrick Forgehand. Colehurst survives, diminished.

### The Third Day at Aldergate
`YT 97 · major · DH`

Deep in the Smoke Year War’s worst fighting, a Colehurst relief column and a rebel vassal force met at Aldergate for what should have been a routine engagement — until, by multiple surviving accounts from both sides, something moved through the middle of the field and neither side had a living soldier left standing within reach of it by the time it was gone. Both commanders, technically enemies, are recorded retreating in the same direction at the same time without a word exchanged. Aldergate is remembered less as a battle than as the moment the Smoke Year War’s own combatants briefly stopped fighting each other and started fighting the same fear instead — a truce that lasted exactly as long as it took both sides to get out of sight of one another.

### Aldergate’s Inheritance
`YT 101 · stub · DH`

In the seasons after Aldergate, the scorched ground around the old battlefield began producing something new — Magma Creepers, never before documented in that region, apparently drawn to or born from the residual heat of a war that had burned the district for the better part of a decade. Local hunters treat the field itself as cursed ground regardless of what’s actually causing it.

### The Concord of the Four Hearths
`YT 104 · vignette · CF · NT 25`

A year after the Dominion falls, all four Houses meet on neutral Thorne ground and create the Shared Roof: cross-House fostering, so no future heir grows up ignorant of the other three. Perrin Thorne formalizes it; Norah Colehurst signs for a diminished House. It is resented — and it holds.

### The Orphans’ Oath
`YT 109 · stub · DH`

In the Smoke Year War’s aftermath, a new hunting lodge was founded specifically for war orphans with nowhere else to go — funded, awkwardly, by a small Colehurst reparations fund nobody involved liked discussing openly. It remains, a century and a half later, one of the more quietly effective Bound chapters in the Fire zone, staffed almost entirely by descendants of people the war itself displaced.

### The Silence Above the Cloud Line
`YT 117 · major · DH`

An expedition of thirteen mapmakers and guides, sent to chart a mountain pass no Faerl survey had reached before, sent one final signal-fire from high ground and were never heard from again. A second party, sent a year later, found their camp intact, equipment untouched, and a single word scratched into a rock face near the site — illegible, worn already by weather, in handwriting nobody could match to any of the thirteen. Summit Titans are the leading explanation, based on ground-disturbance patterns found nearby, though no body has ever been recovered from that pass in the century since. It remains uncharted by choice rather than difficulty.

### Where the Ice Walks
`YT 124 · stub · DH`

A trade route through a mountain ice cave, reliable for two generations, was abandoned within a single season after several consecutive caravans reported losing pack animals to something that moved through the ice itself rather than over it. Frost Creepers and at least one Cave Lurcher are documented in the area since; the route has never reopened.

### The River’s Quiet Refusal
`YT 142 · stub · DH`

A minor Marlow river-trade route, unremarkable for generations, was quietly abandoned after successive boats reported cargo — and, twice, crew — pulled under by something beneath the surface that left no wake and no warning. Undertow Shades are the standing explanation among river-folk, though Marlow’s own official record simply lists the route as “discontinued,” without further comment.

### The Question Yveth Finally Asked
`YT 151 · major · DH`

A minor desert scholar named Yveth Marrelle spent the better part of two decades studying a sealed ruin-ground structure guarded by something the local Farhand called, dismissively, “the glass thing” — a fused, faceted construct that had reportedly killed or maimed everyone who’d tried to force entry for as long as anyone could remember. Yveth didn’t try to force anything. She spent years simply observing it, cataloging its patterns, convinced it was responding to something rather than simply guarding blindly. She was right — and the Glass Shard Golem let her in, alone, on what her own final journal entry calls “the day it finally recognized the question I was actually asking.” She never came back out, and the journal, recovered by a nephew who went looking for her years later, ends mid-sentence. The structure has never let anyone else close since.

### The Forest That Went Quiet at Dusk
`YT 160 · major · DH`

An entire stretch of old-growth Thorne forest, home to two generations of a single logging community, was reduced within a single night to standing ash — trees still upright, bark still intact, but hollowed and grey the way Duskmoor Wood had been over a century and a half earlier. Unlike Duskmoor, this happened fast enough that several loggers sleeping in a nearby camp didn’t wake in time to run. What survivors from the wider settlement describe isn’t a fire, but a cold that arrived first — a stillness in the air that every animal in earshot fled from an hour before anything else happened. The dead ground is marked on modern maps and in modern flavor text alike simply as the Ashen Reach, and no forester has cut timber within a day’s walk of it since. (A Chronicle-Keeper survey notes salamanders thriving at the ancient Embers’ crater but conspicuously absent here, despite near-identical scarring.)

### The Warden No One Thanked
`YT 175 · major · DH`

For longer than any living memory could confirm, a solitary creature — recorded only in fragmentary local testimony as the Ford Sentinel, territorial and dangerous but never aggressive toward travelers who kept their distance — had discouraged anyone from lingering at a particular ford in Thorne territory, driving off the curious and the careless alike for generations. A hunting party finally killed it in YT 175, proud of clearing “dangerous ground” for safer crossing. Five years later, Kester Dray’s horse would stumble on the buried Antem-era technology that killed him, at that exact ford — technology the Ford Sentinel had, without anyone ever understanding why, spent its whole unrecorded life keeping travelers too far back to disturb.

### Ford at Wrenfield
`YT 180 · vignette · CF · NT 21`

Kester Dray’s horse stumbles on a buried Antem-era fragment at a ford in Wrenfield, and an ordinary fall kills him. His Shared Roof foster-brother Halric Colcross is wrongly blamed by commoners who recognized neither man; the court never doubts him. Sorin Dray inherits Dray’s last stable decades.

### The Alpha’s Reach
`YT 188 · stub · DH`

A wolf pack near a Thorne vassal grew, over several seasons, unnaturally large and unnaturally coordinated for the region — territorial hunting patterns closer to a standing army than ordinary pack behavior. The Alpha Wolf responsible was eventually hunted down by a joint effort of several neighboring holdings, none of which had previously agreed on anything else in living memory.

### The Last Night of Reed Hollow
`YT 205 · major · DH`

A modest farming village lost its entire population — some ninety people — over the course of a single night, with survivors from a neighboring settlement describing distant screaming that stopped all at once, followed by silence and a smell like scorched copper that lingered for days. Bound investigators who reached Reed Hollow within the week catalogued the scene in detail no earlier incident affords historians — structures, positioning, timing, all on record. The dispute isn’t over what happened. It’s over what it means that this incident’s signature doesn’t match any prior one closely enough to file as the same threat. Reed Hollow is the incident most modern Bound doctrine points to when arguing demons aren’t one consistent threat but many different ones wearing the same fear — a claim the Reckoning Faith’s mainstream still resists.

### Culling Season
`YT 210 · stub · DH`

A minor river city suffered an outbreak blamed, initially, on bad water — until a physician noticed the sick had one thing in common: recent contact with an unusually aggressive strain of vermin later cataloged as Scavenger Rats, carrying something closer to venom than ordinary filth. The city’s rat-culling ordinances, still enforced today, trace directly back to this outbreak.

### The Hedge Nobody Leaves
`YT 218 · major · DH`

A hedge maze grown by a wealthy Marlow-adjacent family as a garden novelty developed, over several decades of neglect, something considerably worse than overgrowth — travelers who wandered in reported paths that didn’t match on the way back out, and several documented disappearances over a twenty-year span before the family finally condemned the grounds. The Bound’s formal report is unusually precise for a case file — names, dates, entry points, all confirmed against household records. What it can’t settle is why the maze waited two generations of neglect before it started keeping anyone at all. Loop Shades and at least one confirmed Instinct Eater are cited as responsible; the hedges have never been cleared, and burning them was attempted twice and abandoned both times when the crews doing the burning couldn’t agree, afterward, on how long they’d actually been inside.

### The Paramount Lines Fragment
`c. YT 220 – 260 · stub · HG`

One by one the four paramount lines stop producing anyone who can hold them together — Colehurst past ~YT 220, Thorne past ~240, Dray past ~250 (Sorin Dray’s decades being its last stable ones), Marlow past ~260. No single fall; the Houses simply thin into the fractured present.

### A Debt Paid in Skin
`YT 233 · stub · DH`

A minor medical scandal broke when a healer was found using Restorative Leeches — genuinely effective at drawing out infection, but requiring a live host to feed between treatments — on unwitting patients rather than paying for proper feed-stock. The practice wasn’t banned outright, since the leeches’ medical value was real, but licensing law since requires disclosed, consenting hosts only.

### The City Preserved
`YT 245 · major · DH`

A ruin near the Fire zone’s edge, half-swallowed by what locals simply call the amber ground — a resin-like substance that preserves whatever it touches with unsettling, unnatural completeness, tools and bones alike looking freshly used centuries later — has resisted every serious excavation attempt since its discovery. Amber Sentinels patrol its edges with a consistency no naturalist has explained, and at least one expedition lost a member to an Overflow Imp that emerged from beneath the resin itself, apparently disturbed by the digging. What the amber is actually preserving, and why, remains the district’s most persistent unanswered question — the ruin is locally known as Amber City, though nothing about it has ever been confirmed to have been a city at all.

### The Effigy’s Vote
`YT 258 · major · DH`

A minor noble house’s ancestral hall maintained an effigy of its long-dead founder at the head of the great table — tradition, nothing more, until a succession dispute between two feuding heirs saw the effigy reportedly move on its own, siding, by every witness’s account, unmistakably with the elder claimant. Court record confirms the succession dispute, the timing, and the younger heir’s withdrawal within the month — none of that is in question. What the record can’t confirm is whether the effigy actually moved, or whether an heir already losing simply needed somewhere else to place the blame for folding. The effigy — now cataloged among the Seated Ancestors, a creature type few outside the Bound’s stranger case files take seriously — has not moved since, dead weight and painted wood at the head of a table nobody at that hall discusses without lowering their voice first.

### The Basin Keeps Its Own
`YT 266 · major · DH`

A submerged ruin at the bottom of a still, unnaturally deep basin has drawn scattered attention for generations — divers who go deep enough report structures too regular to be natural, and at least one confirmed sighting of a lone, silent figure on the basin’s shore matching descriptions of the Non, watching rather than approaching. A Chronicle Watcher guards whatever’s actually down there; no diver has yet returned with more than fragments, and at least two have not returned at all. Chronicle-Keepers who’ve studied the basin privately favor the theory that it’s an archive of some kind — and privately note that a lone Non watching over an archive nobody Faerl or Antem can read, guarded by exactly the kind of spirit their own order names itself after, is not, historically, the kind of coincidence this world tends to produce.
